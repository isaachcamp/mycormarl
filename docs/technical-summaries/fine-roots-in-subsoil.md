# Do fine roots extend into subsoil?

> **Research date:** 26 October 2026. **Question:** Can the model's fine-root length legitimately extend below topsoil, and does evidence for deep roots justify applying an all-root depth distribution to fine-root length?
>
> **Answer:** Fine roots occur in subsoil across contrasting woody and herbaceous plants; this is supported by direct diameter-resolved measurements, not simply by observations of deep taproots. Their presence supports modelling subsoil fine-root uptake. It does **not** establish that total-root mass, fine-root mass, fine-root length and absorptive length share one depth distribution, or validate a universal value of beta. Those remain separate modelling assumptions.

## Definitions and evidence standard

This note uses **below 30 cm** as an operational subsoil threshold and states actual depths wherever possible. Soil horizons differ among sites; 30 cm is not a universal pedological boundary. Evidence beyond 1 m is identified separately. “Fine” is reported using each paper's diameter or anatomical definition. A diameter cutoff does not itself establish physiological activity.

The following are different observations: roots present at depth; the fraction of root mass or length there; and resource uptake there. A maximum depth alone cannot identify a distribution parameter. Likewise, a measurement of nitrate or water uptake does not establish phosphate influx per unit root length. These distinctions govern the conclusions below.

These measurements span woody plants, perennial grasses and other herbaceous crops. They support a broad capacity for subsoil fine rooting, while leaving substantial species and environmental variation. They do not establish that every species reaches 1 m or that maximum depths are species constants.

## Carrot and other crops: evidence for activity at depth

**Kristensen & Thorup-Kristensen (2004), vegetables.** Minirhizotrons extended to 2.4 m, paired with nitrate-15N injections and six-day uptake measurements. Reported rooting depths were 0.6 m for late sweet corn, **1.3 m for carrot**, and **>2.4 m for white cabbage**. Uptake was near zero below observed rooting depth; root density and tracer uptake were related, and influx per unit length was in the same range across depths and species. This is strong evidence that carrot and cabbage have functioning subsoil root systems rather than only inert deep axes. However, the accessible publisher abstract does not specify a root diameter cutoff or root orders, so it is **supporting functional evidence**, not an independent diameter-resolved fine-root census. [Publisher abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11104-005-0696-y).

**Kristensen & Thorup-Kristensen (2004), catch crops.** The full paper compares Italian ryegrass, winter rye and fodder radish. Roots were followed using minirhizotrons to 2.4 m; final depths were **0.6, 1.1 and >2.4 m**, respectively. Six-day nitrate-15N uptake corresponded to the rooting zones and root density. This corroborates activity below topsoil in both grasses and a dicot, while also demonstrating strong differences among crops grown in the same experiment. Its minirhizotron length measure was not partitioned into a reported fine-root diameter class. [Full paper, abstract, methods and Figures 2–4](https://orgprints.org/32024/1/SSSAJ%202004.pdf).

These tracer studies test **nitrate**, not phosphate. Using them to infer that subsoil root length can be physiologically active is reasonable; using them to assign a phosphate uptake coefficient would require additional evidence.

## A depth-profile source that explicitly targets fine roots

**Jackson, Mooney & Schulze (1997)** explicitly analyse **fine roots ≤2 mm**, using profiles with at least three depth increments to ≥1 m (except permafrost-limited tundra). More than 40 references qualified. Functional-group beta estimates were **0.954 for grasses, 0.975 for shrubs and 0.976 for trees**. This is a more commensurate reference than an unqualified total-root profile. However, it fits **fine-root biomass**; converting its profile to length still assumes a suitable SRL with depth. It also pools substantial ecological variation. [Full text, Methods and Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC23826/), [author-hosted PDF](https://jacksonlab.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj20871/files/media/file/pnas97.pdf).

The PMC HTML equation omits the `1 -` needed for its cumulative-from-surface definition; the table percentages support the increasing CDF used here.

## Fine diameter and absorptive function are separate questions

**Guo et al. (2008)** examined anatomy and mycorrhizal colonization by branch order across **23 temperate tree species**. Absorptive anatomical traits occurred mainly in the first three orders; fourth and higher orders mostly had secondary development. Across the examined branching systems they estimated about 75% of fine-root length to be absorptive, illustrating why a single diameter class can overestimate functional absorptive length. These are anatomical inferences, not depth-resolved uptake measurements, and their average must not become a universal 0.75 multiplier for this model. [Primary full text](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2008.02573.x).

Thus “fine roots do reach subsoil” is well supported, whereas “all represented fine-root length has the same uptake ability everywhere” is a stronger closure assumption.

## What Gerwitz & Page and King establish

**Gerwitz & Page (1974)** describe a depth-distribution function in terms of **total root mass**, including the depth containing 63% of that mass. This makes a concern about transferring its calibration to fine-root length legitimate. The full original study was not verified here, so this note does not assert that every underlying dataset contained all diameter classes. [Publisher's bibliographic abstract](https://www.britishecologicalsociety.org/applied-ecology-resources/document/19740723110/function/).

**King et al. (2003)** model winter-wheat root mass and convert mass to length using a constant specific root weight (4.57 g km−1). Their conclusion explicitly concerns investment in fine roots at depth. Accordingly, the paper should not be dismissed as a purely coarse-root argument, but it is **not independent diameter-resolved empirical validation** of a depth distribution for absorptive fine-root length. [Primary article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4244970/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcg033).

## Implications for the current model

The implementation converts biomass to length using `kfroot × specific_root_length`, then partitions that length with `1 - beta^depth_cm`, normalized to maximum rooting depth. See [roots.py](../../src/mycormarl/plant/roots.py) and [traits.py](../../src/mycormarl/plant/traits.py). The current [parameter register](../model-parameter-register.md) already defines `kfroot` as a fine/fibrous-root fraction, excluding storage roots, and identifies the fraction and SRL as unmatched trait measurements. The issue investigated here is therefore the depth allocation of that fine-root length, rather than an assertion that current code applies fine-root SRL to total belowground mass.

For the present beta = 0.96 and maximum depth D = 150 cm, the fraction below z is

$$
p(z < d \leq D) = \frac{\beta^z-\beta^D}{1-\beta^D}.
$$

Calculated from that equation, **29.23%** of represented length is below 30 cm, **12.80%** below 50 cm and **1.47%** below 100 cm. These are model outputs, not empirical estimates. They show that a 150 cm maximum rooting depth does not imply substantial allocation near that maximum.

The evidence supports the following interpretation and next steps; these are research recommendations, not implemented changes:

1. **Retain the possibility of subsoil fine roots.** Restricting all fine roots to topsoil would contradict direct observations across several functional groups.
2. **Describe the profile as a provisional fine-root length allocation.** Do not claim that all-root mass data validate its shape or its beta for this species mixture.
3. **Match calibration to the quantity represented.** Prefer live fine-root length density by depth, with diameter or order definition and sampling limit recorded. Fine-root mass profiles are useful if the mass-to-length conversion is made explicit; whole-system maximum depth is insufficient.
4. **Keep existence, allocation and activity distinct in sensitivity work.** Vary the profile and maximum depth separately; if effective uptake-active fraction is introduced, keep it conceptually separate from those geometry parameters. Neither the deep Eucalyptus profile nor pooled global tree beta warrants direct substitution for the current default.
5. **Avoid universal functional corrections.** Species, site, depth, age and sampling protocol determine how well a fine-root diameter class approximates absorptive length. Nitrate and water evidence supports activity at depth but does not calibrate the phosphate closure.

## Access and limitations

This is a targeted primary-literature investigation, not a systematic meta-analysis or an estimate of the fraction of all plant species with deep fine roots. Full text was inspected for Laclau, Sutterlütti, Pierret, Jackson, Guo and the catch-crop tracer paper. The vegetable tracer and Chimento papers were supported by publisher abstracts/previews and author institutional records; unavailable methods and per-species numerical results have not been inferred. Gerwitz & Page's full original text remains unverified. King provenance was checked separately alongside this investigation. Species-level coverage is strongest for woody plants and the experimental perennial crops; the grassland measurement pools herbaceous species. The findings resolve biological plausibility more firmly than species-specific numerical calibration.
