# Very-low-phosphorus growth suppression and mycorrhizal transition

**Research date:** 16 September 2026  
**Question:** Are there observations of plant growth suppression under very low P, and is there a measured P concentration at which Australian cluster-rooted plants lose mycorrhizal associations? What is known about AM under very low P?

## Executive answer

The strongest conclusion is that the proposed transition is usually described in the opposite direction. Most Australian Proteaceae are constitutively non-mycorrhizal and use cluster roots under extremely P-impoverished conditions; the literature does not identify a low-P concentration at which an otherwise AM Proteaceae species experimentally switches off AM. Instead, low P generally promotes cluster roots, while **higher P suppresses cluster-root formation**. The evolutionary interpretation is replacement of mycorrhizal acquisition by carboxylate-exuding cluster roots, not an acute physiological loss of AM caused by crossing a low-P threshold.

There are, however, useful quantitative low-P reference points:

* In *Hakea prostrata*, cluster roots were abundant after growth at 0–1 mmol P m^-3 (0–1 µM), strongly reduced at 10 µM, and absent at 100 µM external P. This is a high-P suppression boundary, not a low-P failure boundary.
* In *Macadamia integrifolia*, cluster roots occurred at 0, 2.5 and 5 µM P, declined across that range, and were absent above 10 µM P. Maximum shoot biomass and cluster-root functioning were associated with approximately 0.5–0.8 mg P g^-1 leaf dry mass. Growth itself was lowest at zero P and increased through the low-P range.
* AM symbioses can remain effective at low P: rice and *Rhizophagus irregularis* were studied at 25 µM Pi, where the wild type was well colonized and AM increased P acquisition and yield. A P-starvation-response regulator, PHR2, was required for this response.
* Very low P does not guarantee a positive AM growth response. In a P-fixing soil, wheat inoculated with *Gigaspora margarita* or *G. intraradices* showed growth depressions under low available P in some experiments, despite fungal P transfer or colonization. The response depended on fungal isolate, plant density, season, and the balance between direct-root and fungal P uptake.

Thus, there is no defensible universal “AM disappears below X µM P” threshold. Thresholds are system-specific and should be reported separately for external solution P, soil-test P, plant tissue P, root colonization, fungal biomass, and net plant growth.

## Australian cluster-rooted Proteaceae

### *Hakea prostrata*: a dose response in the low-P range

Shane et al. grew *H. prostrata* hydroponically for eight weeks at 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1, 10, 50 and 100 mmol P m^-3. Cluster-root mass proportion was approximately 0.4 at 0.2–1 µM P, fell to approximately 0.05 at 10 µM, approximately 0.01 at 50 µM, and was zero at 100 µM. In a separate depletion experiment, no cluster roots developed at 10, 50 or 100 µM P, while they were present at 0–1 µM.

The same study found that *H. prostrata* growth was not sharply suppressed in the 0–1 µM range, whereas P uptake was high-affinity and the apparent K_m was approximately 1.2 mmol P m^-3. P toxicity and leaf damage occurred at much higher plant P status: external P of 50–100 µM produced symptoms, and leaf P associated with toxicity exceeded 10 mg P g^-1 dry mass. These values concern P toxicity and cluster-root suppression, not AM loss.

Primary source: [Shane et al. (2004), *Plant, Cell & Environment*, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-3040.2004.01204.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-3040.2004.01204.x).

### *Macadamia integrifolia*: internal leaf P is a stronger marker than external P

Zhang et al. grew seedlings for six months at 0, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50 and 100 µM P. Shoot and root biomass increased from zero P through the low-P range, plateaued around 5–25 µM, and declined at 50–100 µM. Cluster-root numbers were about 33 per plant at zero P, 18 at 2.5 µM, eight at 5 µM, and zero above 10 µM. Leaf P ranged from about 0.24 to 1.9 mg P g^-1 dry mass; maximum shoot biomass occurred at leaf P of about 0.7–0.8 mg g^-1, and the authors identify approximately 0.5–0.8 mg g^-1 as the range associated with greatest cluster-root formation/functioning.

This experiment is particularly useful for modelling because it shows that external µM P is not a universal physiological threshold: the response is mediated by plant P status, and the same nominal external concentration can produce different tissue P depending on uptake and growth.

Primary source: [Zhang et al. (2021), *Frontiers in Plant Science*, DOI 10.3389/fpls.2020.610591](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2020.610591).

As an additional benchmark, *Hakea laurina* showed similar growth at 0 and 6.4 µM solution P but strong growth and root-biomass suppression at 64 µM; mature leaves collected in the field contained only about 0.15–0.27 mg P g^-1 dry mass, and resin-extractable soil P was about 0.11–0.84 mg kg^-1. These are useful environmental/tissue values for extremely P-impoverished Australian systems, but the study did not test AM colonisation or identify a transition threshold.

Primary source: [Yamada et al. (2025), *New Phytologist*, DOI 10.1111/nph.70010](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.70010).

### Is this a loss of mycorrhiza under low P?

For most Proteaceae, the answer is not established as an inducible transition. Primary studies and syntheses describe most Proteaceae as non-mycorrhizal, with cluster roots providing an alternative P-acquisition strategy. Notable exceptions include *Placospermum coriaceum* and *Hakea verrucosa*. In *H. verrucosa*, indigenous AM fungi colonized cluster roots (about 57% root-length colonization in undisturbed ultramafic soil), and inoculation increased shoot P and K, although it reduced growth in steamed soil. This demonstrates that cluster roots and AM can coexist in a special ecological context; it did not establish a P concentration threshold. *Hakea prostrata* is generally treated as non-mycorrhizal. In a recent factorial plant-interaction experiment, no AM colonisation was found in *H. prostrata* across the tested inoculum/P treatments, while *Hakea undulata* cluster-root numbers did not differ between AM and non-AM microcosms.

Primary source for the comparative experiment: [Standish et al. (2021), *Journal of Ecology*, DOI 10.1111/1365-2745.13766](https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2745.13766).

Primary source for the *H. verrucosa* exception: [Boulet & Lambers (2005), *Plant and Soil*, DOI 10.1007/s11104-004-0908-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11104-004-0908-x).

The most accurate interpretation is therefore an evolutionary association between extremely P-poor habitats and non-mycorrhizal, cluster-rooted lineages. The literature located here does not provide a measured low-P concentration that causes an AM-to-non-AM switch within a cluster-rooted species.

## AM fungi at low and very low P

### Low P can actively promote AM development

In rice, *R. irregularis* colonisation was assayed at 25 µM Pi (low P), 200 µM (medium), and 500 µM (high). Wild-type roots were well colonized at 25 µM but hardly colonized at 200–500 µM. Mutants lacking the phosphate-starvation regulator PHR2 were drastically impaired even at 25 µM. PHR2 was required for fungal entry, arbuscule development, AM phosphate uptake, and AM-mediated yield promotion in field soil. This is mechanistic evidence that AM development is wired into the host P-starvation response and that low P can recruit/permit AM rather than suppress it.

Primary source: [Das et al. (2022), *Nature Communications*, DOI 10.1038/s41467-022-27976-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-27976-8).

In *Stevia rebaudiana* inoculated with *R. irregularis*, the highest colonisation occurred at 20 and 200 µM KH2PO4 (73.3% and 67.0% total colonisation). Colonisation fell to 43.3% at 500 µM and 18.4% at 1000 µM; arbuscules fell to 1.48% and 0.4% at those two higher concentrations. This again places the readily observed suppression boundary on the high-P side.

Primary source: [Maya et al. (2020), *Scientific Reports*, DOI 10.1038/s41598-020-73859-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-73859-1).

### AM-associated growth depression under low available P

An important early study I initially missed is Abbott, Robson & de Boer (1984). Using subterranean clover colonized by *Glomus fasciculatum*, they varied P from severely deficient to adequate for mycorrhizal plant growth and measured infected-root length, external hyphal length, and vesicle formation after six weeks. Relieving severe P deficiency increased both infected-root length and external hyphae per unit infected root; further P additions then decreased both measures. At P levels adequate for mycorrhizal plant growth, little external hyphal development or vesicle formation remained. The paper therefore provides strong evidence for a **non-monotonic AM response**: extreme P deficiency can suppress host growth and fungal development, moderate P alleviation can increase AM development, and high enough P can suppress AM structures again. The abstract and accessible metadata do not expose a directly transferable soil-P or solution-P threshold, so it does not supply the missing universal X-value.

Primary source: [Abbott, Robson & de Boer (1984), *New Phytologist*, DOI 10.1111/j.1469-8137.1984.tb03609.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.1984.tb03609.x).

Li et al. studied wheat in a calcareous, P-fixing soil with no additional P and with 20 mg P kg^-1 added as CaHPO4. Depending on fungal isolate and plant density, AM plants were smaller than non-mycorrhizal controls. *G. margarita* caused large depressions in both experiments, while *G. intraradices* caused a depression in one experiment. The authors argue that the depression can arise not only from host carbon cost, but also from suppression of direct-root P uptake combined with inadequate P delivery through the fungal pathway. In the broader work underlying this study, AM wheat could obtain more than half of its P through the fungus while still being smaller, illustrating why biomass response alone is not a sufficient measure of AM function.

Primary source: [Li et al. (2008), *New Phytologist*, DOI 10.1111/j.1469-8137.2008.02410.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2008.02410.x).

A complementary field experiment found that AM fungal biomass increased after P addition at a P-limited site but decreased after P addition at a fertile site. This supports a reversal between fungal P limitation at low fertility and host-carbon limitation at higher fertility, rather than a monotonic P response.

Primary source: [Treseder & Allen (2002), *New Phytologist*, DOI 10.1046/j.1469-8137.2002.00470.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1469-8137.2002.00470.x).

## Practical threshold summary

| System | External P treatment | Observed response | What it does—and does not—establish |
|---|---:|---|---|
| *H. prostrata* | 0–1 µM | Strong cluster-root development; no low-P collapse reported | Low-P operating range for cluster roots; not an AM threshold |
| *H. prostrata* | 10–100 µM | Cluster roots strongly reduced/absent; P toxicity at upper range | High-P suppression boundary |
| *H. verrucosa* | Ultramafic, low-P/high-Ni soil; no P dose series | AM colonized cluster roots (~57% root-length colonization in undisturbed soil) | Exception showing coexistence of AM and cluster roots; no threshold |
| *M. integrifolia* | 0–10 µM | Cluster roots decline from 33 to 0 per plant; growth is lowest at zero P | Species-specific root-trait response; not an AM threshold |
| Rice + *R. irregularis* | 25 µM Pi | Wild type well colonized; PHR2 mutants poorly colonized | AM can function at low P; host starvation signalling is required |
| *S. rebaudiana* + *R. irregularis* | 20–200 µM KH2PO4 | Highest colonisation | Low-P/low-intermediate optimum in this system |
| Subterranean clover + *G. fasciculatum* | Severely deficient → adequate P; exact levels not available in abstract | Severe deficiency limited infected-root and external-hyphal development; moderate alleviation increased both; higher P reduced them | Classic non-monotonic AM response; no universal threshold |
| Wheat + AM fungi | 0 additional P in P-fixing soil | Isolate-dependent growth depression | Low available P can produce negative AM growth response without implying no fungal P transfer |

## Implications for an experiment or model

If the aim is to represent a transition, use separate state variables or response curves for:

1. external labile/solution Pi;
2. plant tissue P, especially young or mature leaf P;
3. AM colonisation and arbuscule abundance;
4. fungal biomass and P status; and
5. net plant growth or mycorrhizal growth response.

The available evidence supports testing a low-P AM regime around 25 µM Pi in controlled culture, with sensitivity runs lower than that if the model requires a harsher boundary. It does **not** support imposing a universal low-P AM-off threshold. For cluster-rooted Proteaceae, the better-supported dose-response is the reverse: cluster-root investment is high at approximately 0–1 µM external P and is suppressed as external/internal P rises, with species-specific suppression around 5–10 µM in the *Macadamia* experiment and around 10 µM in *Hakea*.

## Scope and caveats

* Solution P, soil extractable P, soil total P, and leaf P are not interchangeable.
* “Very low P” is therefore experimental-system specific; 25 µM Pi in a renewed nutrient solution is not equivalent to 25 µM soil-solution Pi in an aged soil.
* AM colonisation, fungal biomass, P transfer, and growth response can move in different directions. Negative growth response does not prove that AM is absent or that P transfer is absent.
* The exact low-P concentration at which a Proteaceae lineage evolved or expresses non-mycorrhizal status remains unresolved in the primary literature located for this note.
