# Low-phosphorus mycorrhizal growth response

**Research date:** 16 September 2026  
**Question:** What evidence is there for a low or negative mycorrhizal growth response under *extremely* low phosphorus, and what mechanisms have been proposed or measured?

## Executive answer

Yes. Primary studies support a low-response regime under the most severe P scarcity, but they do not support a universal external-P threshold. The clearest evidence is for arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) systems:

1. **The fungus can itself become P-limited.** A field test found AM fungal biomass increased after P addition at a P-limited site, while a conceptual model predicted low fungal growth when P is so scarce that both plant and fungus are nutrient limited.
2. **Severe P deficiency can suppress AM development.** In subterranean clover, relieving severe deficiency increased infected-root length and external hyphal length; further P additions eventually reduced fungal structures again. This is a non-monotonic response, not “more P always means less AM.”
3. **Net plant growth can be low or negative despite AM P transfer.** In wheat grown in a highly P-fixing soil, some AM fungi caused large growth depressions under no-added-P conditions. The authors measured substantial fungal contribution to P uptake in related work and argued that growth depression need not mean no P transfer.
4. **The leading mechanisms are coupled rather than exclusive:** direct fungal P limitation; inadequate P delivery relative to the carbon cost of maintaining the fungus; suppression of the plant’s direct root P-uptake pathway; isolate-specific compatibility; and allocation/signalling changes that alter fungal biomass and P transfer.

The most defensible model interpretation is therefore a **low-P limitation regime** rather than an AM-off switch. The location of the regime boundary depends on soil P buffering and chemistry, host, fungal isolate, plant density, carbon supply, and whether the measured variable is plant biomass, fungal biomass, colonisation, P transfer, or tissue P.

## Evidence for low or negative growth response

### 1. AM fungal biomass is predicted and observed to be low at the extreme low-P end

Treseder & Allen (2002) formalised a two-sided limitation model. At intermediate scarcity, the plant is P-limited but can still allocate carbon to a fungus that has enough P to grow; this is predicted to maximise mycorrhizal growth. At the most severe P scarcity, both host and fungus become P-limited, so fungal biomass should be low regardless of host carbon allocation.

Their field manipulation across Hawaiian fertility sites supported the direction of this mechanism: AM biomass increased after P addition at the P-limited site, but P fertilisation reduced AM biomass at a fertile site. The result is consistent with a switch from fungal P limitation at the low-P end to host-carbon limitation at higher fertility.

Primary source: [Treseder & Allen (2002), *New Phytologist*, DOI 10.1046/j.1469-8137.2002.00470.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1469-8137.2002.00470.x). The article’s accessible full text is also available through the [University of California eScholarship repository](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0bj9693x/qt0bj9693x_noSplash_93c75e77bbc5dceee86b8c5b2b1e7f55.pdf).

This is strong evidence for low **fungal growth** at extreme low P, but it is not by itself a measurement of negative plant biomass response.

### 2. Severe deficiency can suppress AM development before P becomes adequate for host growth

Abbott, Robson & de Boer (1984) varied P from severely deficient to adequate for subterranean-clover growth and measured infected-root length, external hyphal length per unit infected root, and vesicle formation after six weeks. Relieving severe deficiency increased infected-root length and external hyphal length. Further P additions then decreased both measures, and P levels adequate for mycorrhizal plant growth were associated with little external hyphal development or vesicle formation.

The result is important because the low-P side is often omitted: AM colonisation and external mycelium are not necessarily maximal at the lowest P treatment. The study does not provide a portable solution-P threshold in the accessible abstract, so it should be used as evidence for curve shape, not numeric calibration.

Primary source: [Abbott, Robson & de Boer (1984), *New Phytologist*, DOI 10.1111/j.1469-8137.1984.tb03609.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.1984.tb03609.x).

### 3. Negative plant growth response can occur in very low-P soil

Li et al. (2008) grew wheat in a highly calcareous, P-fixing soil with either no additional P (P0) or 20 mg P kg^-1 as CaHPO4, inoculated with *Gigaspora margarita* or *Glomus intraradices* (historical names; the latter lineage is now commonly called *Rhizophagus irregularis*). AM plants were sometimes smaller than nonmycorrhizal controls. *G. margarita* produced very large growth depressions in both experiments; *G. intraradices* caused a depression in one experiment. The magnitude depended on fungal isolate and plant density, and the negative response could persist even when AM plants had positive or substantial P uptake.

The authors explicitly challenge the idea that the only explanation is carbon drain. They propose that AM colonisation can suppress direct P uptake by root epidermis and root hairs, while the fungal pathway delivers insufficient P per unit plant biomass. This mechanism can produce a negative growth response without requiring the fungus to transfer no P at all.

Primary source: [Li et al. (2008), *New Phytologist*, DOI 10.1111/j.1469-8137.2008.02410.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2008.02410.x).

### 4. A controlled zero-P treatment can reverse the sign of MGR

Salmerón-Santiago et al. (2023) provide unusually direct evidence for an extreme-P boundary in tomato inoculated with *Rhizophagus irregularis*. With 0 mM NaH2PO4, AM plants had lower height, biovolume, and source-leaf area than the nonmycorrhizal comparison; at 0.065 mM P, the AM growth response was positive. At 1.3 mM P, colonisation was largely suppressed. At 0 mM, colonisation remained high and spore production increased, consistent with a large fungal sink that was not matched by sufficient P return.

The authors also measured changes in soluble sugars and increased expression of carbohydrate-metabolism genes (*SlLIN6* and *SlSUS1*). These observations support an imbalance in C–P exchange as a mechanism, but they do not directly measure carbon flux and therefore do not prove that carbon drain alone caused the growth reduction.

Primary source: [Salmerón-Santiago et al. (2023), *Physiologia Plantarum*, DOI 10.1111/ppl.13857](https://doi.org/10.1111/ppl.13857); [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36648218/).

### 5. P transfer can be substantial even when MGR is weak or negative

Smith et al. (2004) used low-P substrate containing 9.75 µg bicarbonate-extractable P g−1 and compartmented pots with 33P tracing. Growth response varied strongly among flax, medic, tomato, and three AM fungi: some combinations benefited, some were neutral, and one fungus reduced tomato P uptake. Yet fungal P delivery was substantial, with *G. intraradices* supplying close to 100% of plant P in several combinations.

Hetrick et al. (1996) likewise found wheat cultivars that were nonresponsive or negatively responsive even though split-pot 32P measurements showed active fungal P uptake. In one comparison, the nonresponsive cultivar received more labelled P through the fungus than the responsive cultivar. Li et al. (2006) estimated that 50–80% of plant P came through the AM pathway in wheat grown in highly calcareous P-fixing soil, despite no positive biomass response in some treatments.

Together, these studies show that “low MGR” is not equivalent to “no AM P transfer.” A plant can receive P through the fungus but fail to convert that flux into more biomass because of carbon costs, impaired direct uptake, partner mismatch, or other limiting resources.

Primary sources: [Smith et al. (2004), *New Phytologist*, DOI 10.1111/j.1469-8137.2004.01039.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2004.01039.x); [Hetrick et al. (1996), *Botany*, DOI 10.1139/b96-003](https://doi.org/10.1139/b96-003); [Li et al. (2006), *New Phytologist*, DOI 10.1111/j.1469-8137.2006.01846.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2006.01846.x).

### 6. Very low external P can limit fungal growth and induce P-acquisition machinery

Olsson, Hansson & Burleigh (2006) used monoxenic carrot-root-organ cultures with *G. intraradices* and compared low and high external P. Under low P, fungal mycelial biomass was lower in the harvests reported by the study, while transcription of the fungal high-affinity phosphate transporter GiPT was induced. In the same experimental framework, external P was rapidly depleted from the finite low-P compartment.

This is direct controlled evidence for a fungal P-acquisition response under low P. It supports the mechanism that the AM fungus can reach a P-limited growth state, but it does not establish the P concentration at which all AM associations cease or the exact threshold for a negative host growth response.

Primary source: [Olsson, Hansson & Burleigh (2006), *Applied and Environmental Microbiology*, DOI 10.1128/AEM.02154-05](https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.02154-05).

Olsson et al. (2008) further showed strong P plasticity in fungal elemental composition: spores formed under low P contained about 1.3 mg P g^-1 dry mass, compared with about 8.0 mg P g^-1 under high P. These are spore measurements, not a universal structural P requirement for all fungal biomass, but they reinforce that fungal P status is environmentally variable.

Primary source: [Olsson et al. (2008), *Applied and Environmental Microbiology*, DOI 10.1128/AEM.00376-08](https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.00376-08).

## Mechanisms suggested or measured

### Direct fungal P limitation

AM fungi require P for their own metabolism and biomass. At the extreme low-P end, the fungus can become P-limited even while the host is allocating carbon in response to P starvation. This predicts reduced external hyphal growth, lower fungal biomass, lower capacity to explore soil, and potentially reduced P delivery to the plant. The Treseder & Allen field test and the Olsson culture experiments support this mechanism.

### Carbon cost exceeds delivered P benefit

AM fungi are obligately dependent on host carbon. If the fungus continues to receive carbon but P uptake and transfer are too small, slow, or poorly coupled to fungal biomass, the net plant-growth response can be zero or negative. This is the conventional explanation for AM growth depression, but the Li et al. (2008) experiments argue that it should not be treated as the only mechanism.

### Suppression of direct root P uptake

AM colonisation can alter the plant’s direct P-uptake pathway. If root-hair/epidermal uptake is down-regulated or displaced while the AM pathway is weak, total plant P acquisition per unit biomass can fall. Li et al. (2008) identify this as a plausible explanation for very large growth depressions, including cases with low intraradical colonisation or low external hyphal development.

### Host regulation and P-starvation signalling

Low P can actively promote AM competence rather than simply removing an inhibitory signal. In rice, *Rhizophagus irregularis* colonisation and AM-mediated P uptake/yield benefits were observed at 25 µM Pi; the host P-starvation regulator PHR2 was required for efficient fungal entry, arbuscule development, and the AM response. This shows that low P can recruit AM, while also implying that the response depends on an intact host starvation-signalling system.

Primary source: [Das et al. (2022), *Nature Communications*, DOI 10.1038/s41467-022-27976-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-27976-8).

Conversely, high P suppresses AM establishment through host regulation and/or changes in root signals. In *Medicago truncatula*, high P reduced colonisation under 0.0075 versus 3.75 mM Pi treatments, while successful entry events could still develop normally. This is the better-established high-P suppression mechanism and should not be confused with the low-P fungal-limitation mechanism.

Primary source: [Balzergue et al. (2011), *Plant Physiology*, PMC3810610](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3810610/).

### Isolate, host, density, and carbon-demand effects

Li et al. (2008) found that *G. margarita* and *G. intraradices* differed strongly in growth effects, and that plant density altered the size of the depression. This is consistent with a balance among fungal carbon demand, fungal biomass per plant, direct-root competition for P, and the efficiency of P transfer. It argues against assigning a single low-P response to “mycorrhiza” without specifying host and fungal partner.

## What the evidence does not establish

The primary literature located here does **not** establish:

- a universal external solution-P concentration below which AM colonisation or AM-mediated growth always collapses;
- that low plant growth response means no fungal P transfer;
- that colonisation percentage is a proxy for active fungal biomass or plant benefit;
- that lowering fungal P concentration to a particular model value is quantitatively calibrated by any one experiment; or
- that the low-P response is monotonic.

P measurements also need to be labelled carefully: solution Pi, soil-test P, soil-solution P, plant tissue P, fungal tissue P, fungal biomass, colonisation, and net growth response are different observables.

## Implications for a model or experiment

The evidence supports testing a response surface with at least two limitation boundaries:

| P regime | Expected host/fungal state | Evidence-led interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Extremely low available P | Host and fungus can both be P-limited; fungal biomass and net growth response can be low | Treseder & Allen; Olsson et al.; Abbott et al. |
| Low-to-intermediate P | Host remains P-limited, but fungus can grow and deliver P; AM response may be positive | Li et al.; Das et al.; many low-P AM inoculation studies |
| Adequate/high P | Host carbon allocation and AM establishment can be down-regulated | Abbott et al.; Balzergue et al. |

For simulation, it is safer to represent fungal growth, P transfer, plant direct uptake, and plant biomass as coupled but distinct processes. A low-P mechanism can be represented by a finite fungal P pool or P-dependent fungal growth/maintenance requirement, plus a possible reduction in direct-root uptake after colonisation. The exact transition point should be treated as system-specific and explored by sensitivity analysis rather than presented as a literature-derived universal threshold.

## Bottom line

There is credible primary evidence for low AM fungal growth and low or negative plant growth response under extremely low available P. The strongest mechanistic account is that the fungus itself becomes P-limited and cannot provide enough P to compensate for host carbon costs; suppression or replacement of direct-root P uptake can deepen the growth penalty. However, low P can also activate host signalling and increase AM recruitment, so the full response is expected to be non-monotonic and context-dependent.
