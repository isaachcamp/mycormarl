# Soil phosphorus dynamics and access by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi

**Research date:** 29 July 2026  
**Scope:** Terrestrial soils, with emphasis on the timescales and spatial scales
relevant to plant–arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungal (AMF) interactions. Claims are
drawn primarily from experiments, isotope studies, spectroscopy, field
measurements, and chronosequences. “P” denotes phosphorus, “Pi” inorganic
phosphate, and “Po” organic phosphorus.

## Executive answer

Chemical weathering of P-bearing parent minerals, especially apatite, is an
important **source of new P to a developing soil**, but it is not the only
source of soil Pi and usually is not the process that replenishes solution Pi
on a plant-uptake timescale. Atmospheric dust can be an important external
input, especially in old, highly weathered ecosystems
([Chadwick et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1038/17276)). Fertiliser, manure,
sediment and imported biomass are additional inputs in managed or open
systems. Within an established soil, much of the Pi presented to a root or
hypha comes from **desorption or dissolution of pre-existing solid-phase Pi**
and from **mineralisation of Po**, not from fresh bedrock weathering.

The soil solution is therefore a small, rapidly replenished interface rather
than the soil's P reserve. Isotope analyses of 53 soils estimated mean
residence times of minutes to hours for resin- and bicarbonate-extractable Pi,
days to months for NaOH-extractable Pi, and years to millennia for
HCl-extractable Pi ([Helfenstein et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/ejss.12962)).
These are operationally defined fractions rather than discrete molecules, but
the result demonstrates a continuum of exchange rates.

Biota are central to this cycling. Plants and microbes immobilise Pi into
biomass; exudation, grazing, senescence and death return Po; phosphatases and
other enzymes release Pi; and the released Pi may immediately be taken up
again or sorbed to minerals. In one set of forest soils, microbial biomass P
was as much as 53% of total P, an estimated 80% of that microbial pool had a
nine-day turnover time, and 8.5–17.3 kg P ha^-1 could turn over within days
([Achat et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.soilbio.2010.08.023)). Thus a
soil can sustain high gross P flux with little net change in its total P.

AMF are best understood as **spatial foragers and transporters of Pi**. Their
fine extraradical hyphae extend beyond root depletion zones, take up
orthophosphate with fungal phosphate transporters, store and move P in
polyphosphate-rich pools, and release Pi at the root interface
([Maldonado-Mendoza et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1094/MPMI.2001.14.10.1140);
[Kikuchi et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.14016)). They do not have a
demonstrated pathway for taking up large intact organic-P molecules such as
phytate. When experiments show AMF-derived plant P from an organic source, the
organic compound has first been hydrolysed extracellularly and the resulting
Pi taken up.

AMF can sometimes perform or stimulate that hydrolysis. Axenic
*Glomus intraradices* hyphae hydrolysed model phosphate esters and increased P
transfer from phytate ([Koide & Kabir 2000](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1469-8137.2000.00776.x)),
and *Rhizophagus clarus* released acid phosphatase in a compartmented-soil
experiment ([Sato et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/00380768.2014.993298)).
However, another study found most AMF phosphatase activity wall-bound and no
measurable exuded activity ([Joner & Johansen 2000](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0953756299001240)).
Strong experiments also show AMF recruiting, stimulating, and transporting
phosphate-mineralising bacteria
([Zhang et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41396-018-0171-4);
[Jiang et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.17081);
[Wang et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.18642)). Direct fungal enzyme
activity is consequently species-, substrate-, and context-dependent, whereas
**hyphosphere-assisted mineralisation followed by fungal Pi uptake** is the
better-supported general mechanism.

## What “forms of soil P” means

Two classifications are often conflated: actual chemical species and
operational extraction pools.

### Chemical and biological forms

| Form | Examples | Relevant process |
|---|---|---|
| Dissolved Pi | Mainly H2PO4^- and HPO4^2- at soil pH | Immediate substrate for root, microbial and AMF phosphate transporters |
| Reversibly sorbed Pi | Orthophosphate associated with clay edges and Fe/Al (hydr)oxide surfaces | Desorption can buffer solution Pi over minutes to months |
| Mineral Pi | Apatite and other Ca phosphates; Fe- and Al-associated phosphate phases | Dissolution ranges from relatively rapid for some secondary phases to geological for protected primary minerals |
| Occluded Pi | Phosphate enclosed within or strongly protected by mineral coatings and aggregates | Poorly exchangeable on a growing-season timescale |
| Living Po | Nucleic acids, phospholipids, sugar phosphates, metabolites and intracellular polyphosphate in plants, microbes and fungi | Immobilisation, growth, grazing, mortality and lysis |
| Residue and soil Po | Orthophosphate monoesters including inositol phosphates; diesters including DNA and phospholipids; phosphonates and organic polyphosphates | Enzymatic hydrolysis releases Pi, subject to physical and mineral protection |

Solution ^31P-NMR assignments distinguish orthophosphate monoesters, diesters
such as DNA and phospholipids, phosphonates, pyrophosphate and polyphosphate
([Turner et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.2136/sssaj2003.4970)). The method also
shows why “organic P” is not one pool: diesters can hydrolyse readily, whereas
inositol phosphates can be strongly retained by mineral surfaces. Extraction
itself can degrade some compounds, so a reported Po concentration is
method-dependent.

Polyphosphate needs particular care. It consists only of phosphate groups and
is chemically inorganic even when stored inside a living cell. It is therefore
not equivalent to Po, despite being a biological P store.

### Operational fractions

The Hedley sequential extraction separates P by progressively stronger
extractants: resin and bicarbonate fractions are usually interpreted as
relatively labile; NaOH fractions as P associated with Fe/Al phases and soil
organic matter; acid fractions as Ca-associated or primary-mineral P; and the
unextracted residue as strongly protected P
([Hedley et al. 1982](https://doi.org/10.2136/sssaj1982.03615995004600050017x)).
Each extract can contain Pi and Po.

These fractions are useful empirical indices, not unique state variables:

- an extractant can dissolve or transform more than one chemical form;
- “labile” means exchangeable under the extraction conditions and timescale,
  not necessarily dissolved or immediately accessible to a hypha;
- Po and Pi in the same extract are not biologically interchangeable until Po
  is hydrolysed; and
- total, extractable, isotopically exchangeable and solution P should not be
  compared without an observation model.

## Sources, transformations, and losses

### External inputs

1. **Parent-material weathering.** Dissolution of apatite supplies new Pi
   during soil formation. In chronosequences, primary mineral P generally
   declines as it is lost, incorporated into biomass and soil organic matter,
   or transferred to secondary and occluded mineral forms
   ([Crews et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.2307/1938144);
   [Selmants & Hart 2010](https://doi.org/10.1890/09-0243.1)).
2. **Atmospheric deposition.** Asian dust supplied P to Hawaiian forests more
   than 6,000 km from its source, becoming important after local parent
   material was highly weathered
   ([Chadwick et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1038/17276)). Dust is therefore
   not merely a redistribution within the local soil column, although it is
   redistribution at continental scale.
3. **Management and lateral transport.** Mineral fertiliser, imported manure
   or compost, irrigation, flooding, erosion/deposition, and animal movement
   can add P to a modelled patch. Whether litter or manure is an external
   source depends on where its constituent P was acquired.

### Internal cycling

The central short-term loop is:

```text
solid-phase Pi ⇄ solution Pi → plant / fungal / microbial biomass P
                         ↑                    ↓
                         └──── Po mineralisation ← litter, exudates, death
```

The arrows are not equally fast. Sorption can remove a pulse of solution Pi
quickly; desorption replenishes it as organisms draw it down; microbial
immobilisation can compete with plants; and mineralised Pi can be re-sorbed
before plant or fungal uptake. An isotope study across climatic zones found
signatures of repeated biological cycling even in mineral-associated P and
showed that warm, humid soils could have biologically cycled all measured P
pools while comparably old arid soils retained primary-mineral P
([Helfenstein et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05731-2)).

The principal losses are crop or biomass export, leaching of dissolved or
particulate P, and erosion. Strong sorption makes vertical Pi movement slow in
many soils, but macropore flow, erosion, saturation of sorption sites and
colloid transport can invalidate a simple “P never leaches” assumption.

## How P varies in time

There is no single phosphorus timescale.

| Timescale | Dominant changes | Evidence and interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Minutes–hours | Exchange between solution and the most labile solid Pi | Labile-fraction mean residence times were minutes to hours across most of 53 soils ([Helfenstein et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/ejss.12962)). |
| Hours–days | Uptake, local depletion, desorption, microbial immobilisation, enzyme induction and pulses after wetting or residue input | Root-associated solution-P gradients were measurable within a 10-day experiment and differed among plant species ([Wang et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15254121/)). |
| Days–weeks | Turnover of microbial P, fine roots and hyphae; mineralisation and re-immobilisation | The dominant microbial-P pool in the studied forests had an estimated nine-day turnover ([Achat et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.soilbio.2010.08.023)). |
| Weeks–seasons | Plant demand, litterfall, phenology, temperature, moisture, drying–rewetting, fertilisation and biomass export | Seasonal studies find that labile Pi, labile Po and microbial P need not peak together, because uptake, immobilisation and mineralisation respond differently ([Zhao et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foreco.2009.06.047)). |
| Years–centuries | Management legacies, gradual transfer into or out of protected forms, erosion and deposition | After 65 years of cultivation, total P was 29% lower than adjacent pasture in the original Hedley study; microbial activity redistributed P among Pi and Po fractions during incubation ([Hedley et al. 1982](https://doi.org/10.2136/sssaj1982.03615995004600050017x)). |
| Millennia–millions of years | Parent-mineral depletion, occlusion, atmospheric inputs and ecosystem retrogression | A semiarid volcanic chronosequence spanning approximately 3 million years showed declining total and primary-mineral P, an early rise then decline in labile Pi, and increasing Po ([Selmants & Hart 2010](https://doi.org/10.1890/09-0243.1)). |

Total P can consequently appear almost constant over a season while solution
Pi and gross uptake flux change greatly. Conversely, an extraction fraction
can change without an equivalent change in molecular speciation because the
fractions exchange along a continuum.

## How P varies in space

Spatial heterogeneity exists at nested scales:

- **Micrometres to millimetres:** P-rich microsites co-locate variably with
  organic matter, Fe/Al phases, clay and apatite inside aggregates.
  NanoSIMS/µ-XRF/µ-XANES observations found different dominant P forms among
  topsoil and subsoil aggregates and strong within-aggregate heterogeneity
  ([Werner et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-03537-8)).
- **Millimetres around roots and hyphae:** uptake lowers solution Pi near an
  absorber while desorption and diffusion replenish it. Root-axis imaging
  found sub-millimetre zones of both P depletion and accumulation rather than
  one uniform rhizosphere shell
  ([Santner et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-24153-0)).
- **Centimetres to metres:** roots, hyphal networks, decaying litter,
  fertiliser granules, faunal deposits, pores and redox patches create
  hotspots. AMF hyphae can bridge a root-depleted location and a richer patch,
  although they also create depletion zones of their own.
- **Soil profile:** biological inputs and uptake infrastructure often
  concentrate labile and organic P near the surface, whereas parent-mineral
  and mineral-associated forms vary with horizon, hydrology and parent
  material. This pattern is not universal in mixed, eroding or strongly
  leached profiles.
- **Landscape to continent:** vegetation islands, parent material, topography,
  erosion/deposition, land use and dust cause large differences. Along the
  semiarid chronosequence, labile and intermediate P were enriched under tree
  canopies relative to interspaces, while recalcitrant fractions were governed
  more by soil age ([Selmants & Hart 2010](https://doi.org/10.1890/09-0243.1)).

This nesting matters for sampling: a bulk-soil concentration averages over
microsites that roots and AMF encounter selectively. Correlative imaging found
AMF co-located with high-P, low-Al regions and more often with organic-type P
spectra than Al-rich inorganic P
([Keyes et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.17980)).
That is evidence of habitat association, not evidence that intact Po crossed
the fungal membrane.

## What biological cycling means for organic-P levels

Po is both a **stock** and a **throughput pool**. Pi uptake increases living
organic and intracellular P; litter and mortality move it into dead organic
matter; mineralisation decreases Po and increases solution Pi; stabilisation
on minerals or within aggregates can preserve Po; and microbial uptake can
return mineralised Pi to Po almost immediately.

Several consequences follow:

1. **A high Po stock does not imply high availability.** A large stock may be
   dominated by mineral-bound inositol phosphates or physically protected
   residues that enzymes cannot reach.
2. **A low or stable Po stock does not imply low cycling.** Rapid
   mineralisation and equally rapid immobilisation can generate a high gross
   flux but little net pool change.
3. **P limitation does not predict one direction of change.** It can increase
   phosphatase investment and recycling, lowering accessible Po, while tight
   immobilisation and mineral protection preserve or build other Po forms.
   Selmants and Hart found increasing Po and phosphatase activity with soil age
   while labile Pi declined, but this trajectory depends on climate, parent
   material and ecosystem development.
4. **Po proportion and Po amount must be separated.** The fraction of total P
   that is organic can rise even while total P and absolute stocks are being
   lost.
5. **Mortality accounting is decisive.** If dead plant and fungal P leaves a
   model instead of entering litter or microbial pools, the model excludes the
   principal route by which biologically immobilised P returns to soil.

## How AMF obtain P

### The well-supported Pi pathway

AMF benefit comes less from access to a chemically unique Pi species than from
access to **different space** and from transport to the host:

1. Extraradical hyphae explore soil beyond the root surface and enter pores
   inaccessible to roots.
2. High-affinity fungal transporters take up dissolved orthophosphate;
   `GintPT` expression responds to external Pi
   ([Maldonado-Mendoza et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1094/MPMI.2001.14.10.1140)).
3. Pi is incorporated into fungal metabolic pools and polyphosphate for
   storage and long-distance movement. Silencing a fungal aquaporin impaired
   host-directed polyphosphate transport
   ([Kikuchi et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.14016)).
4. Polyphosphate is remobilised and Pi is released near arbuscules; the plant
   takes it up across the periarbuscular membrane.

Carbon supply changes this pathway rather than merely paying for a fixed flux.
In an axenic root-organ culture, sucrose supplied to the mycorrhizal root
increased three-day ^33P uptake by extraradical mycelium from 40.9% to
63.4–64.7% of supplied tracer and altered fungal P storage
([Bücking & Shachar-Hill 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2004.01274.x)).
In a semiarid wheat field, indigenous AMF delivered 6.5–21% of shoot P by day
67 despite low root colonisation
([Ryan et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apsoil.2015.07.002)). These
figures demonstrate that fungal delivery can be substantial, but also that it
varies with host, fungus, soil, time, carbon supply and direct-root uptake.
Root-colonisation percentage is not a reliable substitute for P flux.

AMF gain most where solution Pi is low but a replenishable labile inventory is
distributed beyond the root depletion zone. Strongly occluded P cannot be made
available merely by increasing absorbing area. Hyphae also compete with roots
and free-living microbes, and overlapping hyphal depletion zones eventually
reduce the marginal benefit of additional local length. In the
Schnepf–Roose model, nominal hyphal depletion zones overlapped within about
eight hours under its chosen parameterisation
([Schnepf & Roose 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2006.01771.x));
that number is a model-dependent scale, not a universal biological constant.

### Can AMF take up organic P?

The most defensible answer is:

- **intact organic-P uptake:** not demonstrated as an important AMF pathway;
- **direct access through fungal extracellular hydrolysis:** demonstrated in
  some controlled fungus–substrate combinations;
- **indirect access through plant or hyphosphere microbial hydrolysis:**
  strongly supported and probably more general;
- **subsequent membrane uptake:** Pi, regardless of which organism released
  it.

Evidence for intrinsic fungal mineralisation is real but mixed. In a
microorganism-free split dish, *G. intraradices* hydrolysed model phosphate
esters and transferred more P from phytate to carrot roots
([Koide & Kabir 2000](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1469-8137.2000.00776.x)).
External hyphae of two AMF had high acid-phosphatase activity, but up to 70%
was wall-associated and no exuded phosphatase was detected
([Joner & Johansen 2000](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0953756299001240)).
By contrast, *R. clarus* released detectable acid phosphatase into its
hyphosphere in sterilised Andosol
([Sato et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/00380768.2014.993298)).
These assays differ in fungal species, substrate, soil and the distinction
between cell-wall and freely secreted enzyme.

Phytate illustrates why “phosphatase” is not enough. A 45-day maize rhizobox
experiment supplied 20, 100 or 200 mg phytate-P kg^-1 to a root-free hyphal
compartment. AMF-associated increases in plant P and biomass correlated with
phytase, not acid-phosphatase, activity
([Wang et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2017.00684)). The experiment
shows access to phytate-derived Pi but does not prove that the measured
phytase was secreted by AMF.

Hyphosphere experiments provide a mechanistic alternative. *Rhizophagus
irregularis* exuded fructose that induced phosphatase genes and secretion in
the phytate-mineralising bacterium *Rahnella aquatilis*, increasing phytate
mineralisation and fungal Pi-uptake machinery
([Zhang et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41396-018-0171-4)). AMF hyphae
also transported bacteria to otherwise isolated organic-P patches, enhancing
mineralisation ([Jiang et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.17081)).
Across three field sites and a controlled microcosm, AMF hyphospheres selected
bacterial communities associated with higher phosphatase activity and lower
Po ([Wang et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.18642)).

AMF can therefore obtain appreciable P **from** organic sources, but the
available evidence does not justify a large “organic-P uptake” flux across the
fungal membrane. The quantitative contribution cannot be assigned a universal
percentage: it is limited by Po chemistry, sorption and physical access,
enzyme specificity, pH and moisture, helper microbes, fungal identity, plant
carbon, and competition for the released Pi.

## Implications for MycorMARL

The current `soil_labile_p` state is a defensible **growing-season Pi
availability abstraction**: it combines dissolved and reversibly sorbed Pi,
diffuses according to solution concentration, and permits root and fungal
uptake. It should not be described as total, extractable, mineral, or organic
P. Its closed boundaries and lack of mineralisation mean that it represents a
finite initial labile inventory over the fixed 120-day growing season, not a
complete P cycle.

For the current research question, the clearest modelling consequences are:

1. **Keep root and AMF membrane uptake as Pi uptake.** Do not add a generic
   intact-organic-P Michaelis–Menten term without evidence for a transporter
   and substrate.
2. **Separate uptake from mobilisation.** If Po is added, extracellular
   mineralisation should transfer P from an enzyme-accessible Po pool into the
   existing labile-P pool. AMF may modify that rate through fungal biomass,
   carbon allocation or a hyphosphere-microbe interaction; it should not be
   credited with Po until Pi actually enters fungal biomass.
3. **Return mortality to a pool if recycling is in scope.** Plant, fungal and
   microbial death should create litter/biomass Po; mineralisation then closes
   the loop. The current removal of structural P from the simulated system is
   an explicit alternative hypothesis.
4. **Use at least two Po rates if Po matters.** A single homogeneous Po pool
   conflates rapidly turning-over microbial/residue P with protected phytate
   and mineral-associated Po. A minimal extension could use accessible and
   protected Po, or one Po pool with an explicit accessible fraction.
5. **Treat nonlabile mineral P as a boundary or slow reservoir.** Fresh
   weathering is unlikely to control within-season flux in many established
   soils, but desorption from intermediate Pi fractions can. A slow
   replenishment term should be calibrated to isotope/desorption kinetics,
   not labelled generically as weathering.
6. **Represent heterogeneity before adding chemical detail.** Patchy initial
   labile Pi, patchy Po inputs, depth-varying buffering and litter pulses may
   alter fungal foraging and community assembly more than an additional
   well-mixed pool.
7. **Preserve an observation layer.** Solution concentration, resin P, Olsen
   P, Hedley fractions and total P require different mappings from model
   states.

A useful minimal mass balance would be

```text
labile Pi --uptake--> organism P
    ↑                    |
    |                    | mortality / exudation
    |                    v
    └--mineralisation-- accessible Po ⇄ protected Po
    ↑
slow desorption / dissolution or external input
```

For community-assembly experiments, AMF traits could separately control
hyphal exploration, Pi uptake kinetics, P storage/transfer, intrinsic
phosphatase capacity, and recruitment of mineralising microbes. Collapsing
these into one “organic P uptake” trait would make biologically distinct
strategies observationally indistinguishable.

## Confidence and unresolved questions

Confidence is high that solution and labile Pi turn over much faster than
primary-mineral P; that biota cycle a substantial P flux; that soil P is
spatially heterogeneous; and that AMF take up Pi, store/transport it and can
deliver a large but context-dependent fraction of plant P.

Confidence is moderate that AMF-associated bacteria commonly increase
organic-P mineralisation outside controlled systems; multi-site field evidence
supports the association, but exact causal fluxes remain difficult to assign.

Confidence is low for any universal fraction of AMF nutrition derived from Po,
for substantial intact-Po transport into AMF, or for a single soil-independent
mineralisation rate. Key discriminating experiments would combine labelled
organic substrates, axenic and synthetic-community treatments, measurements of
released Pi, fungal transporter activity, and time-resolved P transfer to the
host.
