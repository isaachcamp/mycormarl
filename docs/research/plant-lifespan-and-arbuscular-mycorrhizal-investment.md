# Plant lifespan and investment in arbuscular mycorrhiza

**Research date:** 29 July 2026  
**Scope:** Whether plant lifespan or life-history category predicts (1) the
tendency to form arbuscular mycorrhiza (AM), (2) root colonisation,
mycorrhizal dependence or responsiveness, and (3) bidirectional resource
transfer between plants and arbuscular-mycorrhizal fungi (AMF). Evidence is
drawn primarily from original experiments, field surveys, tracer studies and
phylogenetically informed comparative analyses.

## Executive answer

There has been research on the question, but it does **not** support a universal
rule that longer-lived plants are more likely to form AM or always invest and
receive more.

Some of the clearest comparative experiments do support a perennial advantage.
In congeneric grass pairs, perennial *Panicum virgatum* and *Bromus inermis*
were more responsive and dependent on AMF than their annual congeners, although
the result depended on fungal isolate and soil phosphorus (P)
([Boerner 1992](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00203289)). In greenhouse tests of 95
tallgrass-prairie species, annuals generally had lower colonisation and weaker
growth responses than native perennials
([Wilson & Hartnett 1998](https://doi.org/10.2307/2446507)). A Chihuahuan
Desert survey likewise found much less AM colonisation in annual species, whose
fine roots were also thinner, than in perennials
([Collier et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-1963(03)00031-4)).

But these patterns are not general. Among 18 field-grown species spanning
Fabaceae, Asteraceae and Poaceae, annuals had **higher**, not lower, root
colonisation than perennials, alongside higher specific root length and root N
and lower root tissue density
([Roumet et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2006.01667.x)).
In a common-soil experiment with 27 species, colonisation did not differ
between annuals and perennials; functional group and nutrient preference were
more informative ([Eschen et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00572-012-0451-1)).
An inoculation experiment covering 646 Brassicaceae taxa also found no
annual–perennial difference, and the observed internal hyphae and vesicles
lacked arbuscules, making them unlikely to represent functional nutrient
exchange ([DeMars & Boerner 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0367-2530(17)30711-9)).

The broadest comparative result is especially cautionary. Hempel et al.
compiled mycorrhizal status for 1,758 Central European plant species and used
phylogenetically corrected analyses. Lifespan was associated with mycorrhizal
status in the full dataset, but **not in the higher-confidence core dataset**;
the previously reported excess of obligately mycorrhizal perennials could not
be confirmed ([Hempel et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1890/12-1700.1)).

Evidence that lifespan itself controls the **magnitude of C-for-nutrient
transfer is much weaker still**. I found no experiment that independently
manipulated lifespan while directly measuring both plant-to-fungus C and
fungus-to-plant P or N flux. Direct tracer studies instead show that exchange
varies with current photosynthesis, season, soil P, host and fungal identity,
and host genotype. These factors can correlate with lifespan but are not
equivalent to it.

The defensible synthesis is that perenniality is a useful ecological predictor
in some grassland and desert floras, but not a reliable causal switch. Longer
time can permit payback and infrastructure reuse; current demand, root
strategy, nutrient supply and partner identity are better-supported controls
of realised transfer.

## Keep five different response variables separate

| Quantity | What it means | What it does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|
| Association status or frequency | Whether a species is recorded as non-, facultatively or obligately mycorrhizal, or the frequency of records showing AM | Colonisation intensity in a particular individual; benefit; or resource flux |
| Colonisation intensity | Fraction of sampled root length containing fungal structures, sometimes separated into hyphae, arbuscules and vesicles | The amount of active exchange; arbuscules can differ in transporter activity |
| Mycorrhizal dependence | How poorly a plant grows without AMF, under defined conditions | The absolute amount received from fungi, or whether the interaction is beneficial in another environment |
| Mycorrhizal responsiveness | Biomass, P, survival or reproductive change caused by inoculation or suppression of AMF | Dependence: a plant can be nondependent yet respond, or highly colonised yet show no biomass gain |
| Resource flux | C, lipid or sugar moving to AMF, or fungal P, N or water reaching the plant per unit time or over an interval | Lifetime benefit unless the measurement is integrated over the relevant lifecycle |

This separation explains several apparent contradictions. Roumet et al.
measured field colonisation, Boerner measured growth response and dependence,
and Hempel et al. analysed categorical species status. They did not measure the
same biological property.

## Cross-species lifespan comparisons

### Evidence consistent with greater perennial reliance

**Congeneric grasses.** Boerner's design is unusually relevant because it
compared sympatric annual and perennial species within *Panicum* and *Bromus*,
under two P levels and with two *Glomus* isolates. The perennials were more
responsive and dependent overall. However, neither *Bromus* species responded
strongly to one isolate, and annual responsiveness appeared at low but not
high P. Lifespan therefore predicted outcomes in this small comparison, but
fungal identity and nutrient context changed the result
([Boerner 1992](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00203289)).

**Tallgrass-prairie flora.** Wilson and Hartnett grew 36 grass and 59 forb
species with and without AMF. Annuals were generally less colonised and less
responsive than native perennials, but C3 versus C4 phenology was a strong
predictor among grasses, and the colonisation–growth-response relationship
differed between legumes and nonlegumes
([Wilson & Hartnett 1998](https://doi.org/10.2307/2446507)). The study provides
broad original experimental support for a life-history pattern, but not a
clean lifespan effect independent of taxonomy, photosynthetic pathway,
phenology or functional group.

**Desert annuals and perennials.** Collier et al. examined 17 dominant
Chihuahuan Desert species. Eighty-seven percent of annual-root segments lacked
AM hyphae, whereas only 10% of perennial-root segments did; 61% of perennial
segments had more than 80% of their length colonised. Annuals also had thinner
fine roots. Within 15 of 17 species, colonisation presence did not itself
explain root diameter, suggesting that the diameter difference was a host trait
rather than fungal swelling
([Collier et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-1963(03)00031-4)).
This is a strong regional association, but the authors' “dependency” inference
was based mainly on colonisation and morphology rather than an AMF-exclusion
growth test or tracer flux.

### Counterexamples and null results

**Annuals can be more colonised.** Roumet et al. measured entire root systems
of 18 herbaceous species grown in the same field and represented annual and
perennial Fabaceae, Asteraceae and Poaceae. Annuals had higher colonisation
across these families. Their roots were also thinner/more acquisitive: higher
specific root length and N, and lower tissue density
([Roumet et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2006.01667.x)).
This directly reverses the simple “perennials are more colonised” prediction
and shows why root economics and growing conditions matter.

**No annual–perennial difference in a common soil.** Eschen et al. grew
seedlings of 27 species in soil from a nutrient-rich set-aside field across a
carbon-addition gradient intended to reduce nutrient availability. Colonisation
did not differ by annual versus perennial life history. Grasses were less
colonised than forbs and legumes, and plant Ellenberg N preference affected the
response to carbon addition
([Eschen et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00572-012-0451-1)).

**Lifespan does not restore a lost functional symbiosis.** DeMars and Boerner
inoculated 646 Brassicaceae taxa. About 19% showed some internal fungal
development, but annuals and perennials did not differ. No arbuscules were
found in 3,230 root segments, so apparent entry did not demonstrate a
functional AM association
([DeMars & Boerner 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0367-2530(17)30711-9)).
Phylogenetically conserved host machinery can therefore dominate lifespan.

### Comparative datasets and phylogeny

Hempel et al. assigned 1,758 Central European species to obligately
mycorrhizal, facultatively mycorrhizal and nonmycorrhizal states and analysed
life-history and habitat traits with phylogenetic correction. The full dataset
gave a lifespan association (chi-square 14.36, *P* < 0.01), but the relationship
was not significant in the restricted core of better-supported status records.
The authors explicitly concluded that the UK pattern of obligate mycorrhizas
being overrepresented among perennials was not confirmed for Central Europe
([Hempel et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1890/12-1700.1)). This is the best
reason not to treat lifespan as a generally validated predictor of AM status.

Reanalysis of Wilson and Hartnett's 95-species experiment found phylogenetic
signal in both colonisation and growth response. Models using phylogeny and
models using phenotypic plus taxonomic information predicted responsiveness
similarly well ([Reinhart et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1461-0248.2012.01786.x)).
Across a much larger inoculation database, recent plant-species divergence,
plant–fungal matching and study context explained substantial variation in AM
growth response; life history was tested alongside functional group,
fertilisation, domestication and experimental conditions rather than emerging
as a universal master variable
([Hoeksema et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-018-0120-9)).

Root-trait synthesis supplies a plausible confounder. Across hundreds of
species, thicker roots and larger cortex fractions aligned with greater
mycorrhizal colonisation along a phylogenetically conserved “collaboration”
axis, whereas root lifespan aligned with a different tissue-conservation axis
([Bergmann et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aba3756)). Plant
lifespan, root lifespan and fungal outsourcing should therefore not be treated
as interchangeable traits.

## Ontogeny and phenology within a plant

Plant lifespan comparisons ask whether an annual species differs from a
perennial species. Ontogenetic studies instead ask how one plant–fungus
association changes as it develops. Seasonal studies additionally combine
plant stage with temperature, light, water and community composition.

### Establishment takes time

Colonisation is initially constrained by encounter and fungal growth. In onion,
*Glomus deserticola* colonisation began three days after inoculation and
reached 50% root length after 21 days; root age and inoculum position also
changed colonisation of onion, cotton and pepper
([Afek et al. 1990](https://doi.org/10.21273/JASHS.115.6.938)). In sunflower
with *Rhizoglomus irregulare*, no structures were seen at day 2, 7.3% of root
length was colonised at day 4, and roughly 49–55% at days 16–20
([Vangelisti et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-017-1211-1)).
In another experiment, two AMF were equally abundant after two weeks but
diverged strongly by weeks 4 and 10; arrival order also affected later
colonisation ([Werner & Kiers 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.13092)).

These are elapsed-time and priority effects, not proof that an evolved
long-lived host strategy causes greater investment. They do show that a short
lifecycle can finish before a slowly establishing pairing reaches its mature
state.

### Colonisation, benefit and reproduction can change at different times

In *Plantago major*, metabolic and photosynthetic effects became more
pronounced between 20 and 62 days after inoculation, and at day 62 many foliar
changes could not be reproduced by P addition alone
([Schweiger et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1094/MPMI-05-14-0126-R)).

In three soybean genotypes measured at flowering and podding, AMF-induced
phosphate-transporter expression and plant P responses depended on both
genotype and stage. One genotype showed significant shoot biomass and total-P
benefits at podding that were not yet expressed the same way at flowering
([Yang et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.7671/j.issn.1001-411X.202010024)).
The experiment measured tissue P, not isotope-resolved fungal delivery, but it
supports a delayed-benefit mechanism.

AMF can also alter the host's lifecycle rather than merely respond to it. In a
112-day experiment with *Medicago truncatula*, six AMF species differed in
their effects on flowering and lifecycle completion. Under low P, the
nonmycorrhizal plants did not flower, and only four fungal treatments enabled
completion by harvest. Under high P, nearly all treatments completed. Root
colonisation, total plant P and reproductive response did not map onto one
another uniformly across fungal species
([Liu et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/jpe/rtx023)).

Field sampling of the perennial *Antennaria dioica* found more hyphal,
vesicular and arbuscular colonisation during flowering than after seed
production, while reproductive and nonreproductive individuals also differed
([Vega-Frutis et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.4161/psb.23445)). This is
consistent with phenological carbon and nutrient demand, but does not isolate
age from reproductive state or environment.

## What direct resource-transfer measurements show

### Resource movement is rapid and dynamic

Using ^13C-labelled glucose and ^13CO2, Olsson et al. detected plant carbon in
AMF mycelium within one day and found rapid translocation toward extraradical
mycelium ([Olsson et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.71.5.2592-2599.2005)).
Thus exchange does not require a perennial-length interval once a functional
interface and network exist.

### Season changes active C flow without necessarily changing colonisation

Lekberg et al. pulse-labelled established, coastal *Hypochaeris radicata*
plants with ^13CO2 in February, July and October. Recent C allocation to fine
roots and AMF storage lipid varied seasonally and mirrored plant assimilation,
but structural lipid and microscopy-based colonisation did not; colonisation
remained above 80% throughout the year
([Lekberg et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11104-012-1534-7)).

This is direct evidence that **active C transfer and visible colonisation can
be decoupled**. It is not a cross-species annual–perennial test: it follows a
perennial host through seasons, so plant phenology, temperature and light
change together. A later temperate-grassland ^13CO2 study likewise found that
recent plant C flow to AMF peaked in warmer months
([Birgander & Olsson 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11104-021-04854-8)).

### Host and environment alter the exchange ratio

In intact grassland communities moved to a glasshouse, paired ^32P and ^13C
measurements showed that P-for-C exchange differed between co-occurring grasses
and blanketflower and between source soils differing about tenfold in labile
Pi. Grasses contained more recent ^13C in root-associated AMF, while fungal
^32P delivery and exchange ratios changed with soil context
([Lekberg et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.19501)). These plants were
not selected to manipulate lifespan, so the study demonstrates host and
environmental variation rather than a lifespan effect.

High-resolution root-organ experiments provide an even cleaner test of host
identity. Estimated C and P fluxes were approximately proportional through
fungal network development, and switching carrot root genotype shifted the
C:P exchange rate more strongly than switching among the tested fungal strains
([Bisot et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2512182123)). Genotype can
therefore alter exchange within one host species, without a lifespan
difference.

These studies support time-varying exchange, not the proposition that a plant
with a longer potential life transfers more. A perennial may accumulate more
lifetime flux simply by operating longer while having a lower, equal or higher
instantaneous rate.

## Why longer life could matter without being a universal predictor

Several mechanisms make a lifespan effect plausible:

1. **Establishment and payback lag.** Carbon is spent before extensive
   colonisation and soil exploration exist. A longer-lived plant has more time
   to recover that fixed cost, especially with a slowly colonising fungal
   partner.
2. **Reuse of infrastructure.** Persistent roots or recurring ramets can meet
   established external mycelium. AMF mycelium from shootless host systems
   remained viable and able to colonise new plants for up to five months
   ([Pepe et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-28354-5)). Fungal
   persistence is therefore partly uncoupled from one host's lifespan.
3. **Different root economics.** Thick, cortex-rich roots may outsource soil
   exploration, while thin, high-specific-length roots can forage directly.
   These traits sometimes correlate with perenniality but do not always do so.
4. **Reproductive schedule.** Annuals must redirect resources to seeds within
   one season. That may reduce the time over which a delayed nutrient return
   improves vegetative biomass, yet rapid AMF establishment can advance
   flowering and seed production.
5. **Network continuity.** An annual seedling entering a living common
   mycorrhizal network faces a different lag from one relying on spores. Land
   management, host neighbours and arrival order can overwhelm its intrinsic
   lifespan category.

These mechanisms predict interactions, not a main effect. Greater perennial
benefit is most plausible with slow colonisation and delayed payback. With fast
colonisation or a pre-existing network, annuals can still benefit substantially.

## Confounders that should not be labelled “lifespan effects”

- **Phylogeny and symbiotic capacity.** Brassicaceae and other lineages may
  lack a functional AM programme regardless of being annual or perennial.
- **Successional status.** Late-successional prairie plants often respond more
  strongly to AMF, but succession also covaries with growth rate, disturbance,
  nutrient economy and perenniality
  ([Koziol & Bever 2015](https://doi.org/10.1890/14-2208.1)). It is not a
  synonym for lifespan.
- **Woodiness.** A tree is long-lived, but many temperate trees are
  ectomycorrhizal rather than AM. Woody versus herbaceous comparisons mix
  mycorrhizal type, architecture, size and phylogeny with longevity.
- **Root traits.** Diameter, cortex fraction, root hairs, specific root length
  and root tissue density affect direct versus fungal acquisition.
- **Nutrient and water environment.** P supply can switch responsiveness and
  the preferred P-acquisition pathway; water and N can change the value of
  other fungal services.
- **Host and AMF identity.** Different pairings establish at different speeds
  and deliver different amounts of P for a similar visible colonisation.
- **Inoculum and network persistence.** Spores, colonised root fragments and
  living hyphae impose different establishment delays.
- **Observation duration.** A harvest before payback makes AMF appear costly;
  a later harvest may reveal nutrient or reproductive benefits. Conversely,
  late cumulative biomass can conceal an early cost.

## Implications for MycorMARL

MycorMARL's fixed **growing season** is 120 days (4,800 environment steps at
0.025 days per step). That horizon is biologically long enough for many
annual-host AM associations to establish and affect reproduction: controlled
studies reached substantial colonisation within 2–3 weeks, and AMF changed
*Medicago* lifecycle completion within 112 days. The model should therefore
not assume that an annual plant has too little time to trade.

The evidence supports the following modelling choices:

1. **Do not encode lifespan as a direct multiplier of trade.** There is no
   defensible universal coefficient such as “perennials trade more.” Treat
   lifespan as a boundary condition on how long costs and benefits can accrue.
2. **Represent establishment explicitly if lifecycle questions matter.** A
   colonisation or interface-capacity state that ramps over days to weeks is
   more evidence-based than instantaneous full exchange. Its rate should
   depend on AMF identity, inoculum/network state and host traits.
3. **Separate instantaneous flux from cumulative return.** The same daily
   trade policy can produce different lifetime outcomes solely because an
   organism remains operational for longer. Record cumulative plant C paid,
   fungal P delivered, and time to net payback.
4. **Keep colonisation separate from exchange activity.** Persistent
   colonisation can coexist with low recent C flow. If colonisation is modelled,
   an exchange-capacity or activity term should still respond to current plant
   carbon supply and nutrient demand.
5. **Let reproduction compete with symbiotic investment.** The existing
   physical action allocation between trade, growth, reproduction and reserve
   already provides the right conceptual trade-off. An annual strategy can
   redirect allocation toward reproduction late in the growing season; a
   hypothetical perennial strategy can value reserve or infrastructure beyond
   day 120 only if continuation between seasons is represented.
6. **Distinguish plant survival from fungal persistence.** When the plant
   becomes non-operational, external mycelium need not disappear instantly.
   A persistent-network state would allow a successor or neighbouring plant to
   bypass some establishment lag. Without such a state, MycorMARL is modelling
   a fresh bilateral pairing each growing season.
7. **Test lifespan as an emergent strategy.** A useful experiment would vary
   plant termination/reproduction schedules while holding uptake traits and
   partner identities fixed. Then vary establishment delay and network carryover.
   This would test when longer operational life favours trade without asserting
   the answer in a parameter.

A minimal hypothesis grid for the 120-day model is:

| Lifecycle treatment | AM establishment | Network at day 0 | Expected question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual, fixed death after reproduction | Fast vs slow | Absent | Can trade repay before reproductive commitment? |
| Annual | Fast vs slow | Present | Does inherited fungal infrastructure remove the perennial advantage? |
| Seasonally dormant perennial | Fast vs slow | Root/fungal state carried over | Does reuse increase cumulative return across seasons? |
| Same lifespan, contrasting root strategy | Matched | Matched | Is an apparent lifespan result actually direct-root versus fungal foraging? |

The current single-season lifecycle cannot estimate a perennial's lifetime
advantage unless biomass, reserves, reproductive value and fungal
infrastructure carry over between growing seasons. Within the current scope,
the strongest defensible question is narrower: **given 120 days, how do
establishment delay and reproductive timing alter the realised return on
trade?**

## Confidence assessment

| Conclusion | Confidence | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Lifespan is not a universal determinant of whether plants form AM | High | Large phylogenetically corrected comparison loses the signal in its high-confidence core; large within-family Brassicaceae test is null |
| Perennials are more dependent/responsive in some grassland and desert systems | Moderate–high | Congeneric experiment, 95-species experiment and regional field survey agree, but traits and taxonomy remain partly confounded |
| Perennials are always more highly colonised | High confidence this is false | Common-field annual counterexample and 27-species null result |
| Longer lifespan causes greater instantaneous or proportional C↔P/N transfer | Low / not established | No direct lifespan manipulation with bidirectional flux measurement found |
| Exchange and benefit change over ontogeny and season | High | Time-course physiology, tissue-P studies and direct ^13C measurements |
| Visible colonisation is an adequate proxy for active transfer | High confidence this is false | Seasonal ^13C flow changed while colonisation stayed above 80% and stable |
| Establishment lag and network persistence can mediate lifecycle effects | Moderate | Strong evidence for colonisation lags and post-host mycelial persistence; direct annual–perennial causal test is missing |
