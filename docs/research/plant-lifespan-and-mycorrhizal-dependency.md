# Plant species lifespan, mycorrhizal status, flexibility, and dependency

**Research date:** 1 September 2026  
**Question:** Is plant species lifespan related to mycorrhizal dependency, status,
or flexibility, and what does colonisation measure functionally?  
**Scope:** Primary sources only: Moora et al. (2025), Moora (2014), original
comparative experiments, field studies, and original comparative datasets. The
main evidence concerns arbuscular mycorrhiza (AM), with other mycorrhizal types
included where they clarify status or evolutionary comparisons. The scope is
explicitly broader than Moora-defined dependency, but the categories below are
kept separate rather than treated as interchangeable.

## Concise conclusion

There is a relationship in some datasets, but no general law that longer-lived
plants are more mycorrhiza-dependent. The most direct lifespan experiments
found greater AM growth response/dependency in perennial than annual grasses,
especially under low phosphorus and with particular fungal isolates
([Boerner 1992](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00203289); [Wilson & Hartnett
1998](https://doi.org/10.2307/2446507)). A Chihuahuan Desert field survey also
found lower AM infection in annuals than perennials, but inferred dependency
mainly from colonisation and root morphology rather than from mycorrhizal versus
nonmycorrhizal growth differences ([Collier et al.
2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-1963(03)00031-4)).

Those results are not universal. Annuals had higher root colonisation than
perennials in a common field comparison of 18 species ([Roumet et al.
2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2006.01667.x)); annuals and perennials
did not differ in a 646-taxon Brassicaceae inoculation experiment, and the
fungal structures observed lacked arbuscules ([DeMars & Boerner
1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0367-2530(17)30711-9)). In a phylogenetically
corrected dataset of 1,758 Central European species, lifespan was associated
with categorical mycorrhizal status in the full dataset but not in the
higher-confidence core dataset ([Hempel et al.
2013](https://doi.org/10.1890/12-1700.1)).

The defensible answer is therefore: **lifespan/annuality can predict realised
mycorrhizal benefit or field association in particular floras and environments,
but evidence for a general relationship with Moora-defined dependency,
status, or flexibility is weak, heterogeneous, and often proxy-based.** Root
economics, phylogeny, phenology, phosphorus supply, fungal identity, and
disturbance are plausible co-determinants. No primary study identified here
manipulated plant lifespan while measuring the resulting Moora-defined growth
difference or flexibility across otherwise matched species.

## Operational definitions: status, flexibility, dependency, and colonisation

Moora et al. (2025) define **mycorrhizal dependency** as “the difference in
plant growth between mycorrhizal and nonmycorrhizal treatments.” Their
framework places type, status, dependency, and root colonisation rate among
traits describing the niche optimum, while flexibility and variation in
colonisation describe niche width ([Moora et al.
2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44358-025-00030-3), abstract and glossary).
The underlying definitions are developed explicitly by Moora (2014):

* **Status:** whether roots are consistently colonised in nature: obligately
  mycorrhizal (OM), sometimes/facultatively mycorrhizal (FM), or never
  mycorrhizal (NM). Status is a species-level frequency/consistency category,
  not the percentage of root length colonised in one individual.
* **Flexibility:** the ability to grow with or without mycorrhiza depending on
  local conditions. Under this definition FM species are the flexible category;
  OM and NM species are treated as inflexible because their participation is
  consistently present or absent in nature ([Moora 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/jvs.12177)).
* **Colonisation rate:** an observation of fungal structures or colonised root
  length in a specified host, place, time, and treatment. In Moora et al. (2025),
  its mean is used as a proxy for community dependence and its among-condition
  variation as a proxy for plasticity; those uses do not turn colonisation into
  dependency or flexibility at the individual-plant level.

For this report:

| Quantity | Evidence supplied by it | What it cannot establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Moora-defined dependency | Difference in growth between inoculated/mycorrhizal and nonmycorrhizal treatments | Lifetime reliance, resource flux, or causation by lifespan |
| Responsiveness/benefit | Growth, biomass, P, survival, or reproduction changes after AMF exposure | That the plant cannot grow without AMF |
| Colonisation | Fungal structures or fraction of root length colonised | Active exchange or growth benefit |
| Obligacy/status | A categorical classification such as obligately, facultatively, or nonmycorrhizal | The magnitude of growth response in a specified environment |
| Flexibility | Demonstrated or inferred capacity to grow with and without mycorrhiza across local conditions; in Moora’s operational category, usually FM | A single colonisation value, or evidence that a plant can merely be colonised |

“Dependency” and “responsiveness” are often used inconsistently in older AM
literature. A growth difference can operationally measure both, but the
interpretation depends on the treatment contrast, nutrient regime, harvest
time, and whether “dependency” means a quantitative effect or an obligate
status.

## Direct evidence involving annual versus perennial plants

### Evidence for a perennial advantage

Boerner compared sympatric congeneric annual and perennial pairs in *Panicum*
and *Bromus*, with two phosphorus levels and two *Glomus* isolates. The
perennials *Panicum virgatum* and *Bromus inermis* generally had larger growth
responses and greater dependency than annual congeners when inoculated with
*G. intraradices*. Annual responses were detectable mainly under low P, and
the result changed with fungal isolate. This is the closest of the identified
studies to a direct test of lifespan against Moora-defined dependency, but it
contains few species and a strong interaction with P and fungal identity
([Boerner 1992](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00203289)).

Wilson and Hartnett grew 95 tallgrass-prairie species with and without AMF.
Native perennial warm-season grasses and perennial forbs generally benefited,
whereas annuals were generally less responsive and less colonised. However,
grass phenology and C3/C4 grouping were also strongly associated with response,
and the colonisation–response relationship differed between legumes and
nonlegumes. Thus the result supports an annual–perennial pattern in that flora,
not an isolated lifespan effect ([Wilson & Hartnett
1998](https://doi.org/10.2307/2446507)).

Collier et al. surveyed 17 dominant Chihuahuan Desert species and reported
substantially less AM infection in annual than perennial roots, with annuals
also having thinner fine roots. Their two resulting groups—short-lived annuals
with thin roots and low AM dependency versus longer-lived perennials with
thick mycorrhizal roots—are ecologically informative, but the study did not
estimate dependency as a mycorrhizal-minus-nonmycorrhizal growth difference.
The title’s “dependency” is therefore stronger than the direct measurement
supports under Moora’s definition ([Collier et al.
2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-1963(03)00031-4)).

### Counterevidence and null results

Roumet et al. measured whole-root traits and AM colonisation in 18 field-grown
species from three families in Argentina. Annuals had **higher**, not lower,
AM colonisation than perennials, along with higher specific root length and
root nitrogen and lower root tissue density. This is a direct counterexample
to treating perenniality as a universal predictor of colonisation, although it
did not measure Moora-defined growth dependency ([Roumet et al.
2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2006.01667.x)).

DeMars and Boerner inoculated 646 Brassicaceae taxa. Only 18.9% showed some
internal fungal development; annuals and perennials did not differ, and none
of 3,230 examined root segments contained arbuscules. The observed hyphae and
vesicles therefore did not demonstrate a functional nutrient-exchange
association. This is a null result for lifespan predicting apparent fungal
development, and a warning against interpreting colonisation as dependency
([DeMars & Boerner 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0367-2530(17)30711-9)).

## Large comparative evidence: status is not dependency

Hempel et al. assembled mycorrhizal-status data for 1,758 German-flora species
and analysed associations with life-history traits using phylogenetically
corrected models. Their lifespan variable was annual, biennial, or perennial;
their response was categorical status: obligately mycorrhizal, facultatively
mycorrhizal, or nonmycorrhizal. Lifespan was significant in the full dataset
(chi-square 14.36, *P* < 0.01), with annuals under-represented among the
obligately mycorrhizal category in the follow-up deviations. The relationship
was not significant in their restricted core dataset, and the authors noted
that plant traits were tightly interrelated and that status records and
publication effort were uneven ([Hempel et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1890/12-1700.1),
especially Table 2, Table 3, and the discussion).

This study supports a conditional association between lifespan and **status**
in one regional flora. It does not establish a relationship between lifespan
and Moora-defined **dependency**, because it contains no matched mycorrhizal /
nonmycorrhizal growth treatments for the species.

### Evidence specifically relevant to flexibility

The best large-scale flexibility analysis located here is not a lifespan test.
Zobel et al. classified species as flexible when some published records showed
mycorrhizal roots and others reported nonmycorrhizal status; species with only
mycorrhizal records or only nonmycorrhizal records were classified as
nonflexible. In 2,144 species, 661 (31%) were putatively flexible, 1,142 (53%)
nonflexible mycorrhizal, and 334 (16%) nonflexible nonmycorrhizal. Flexible
species had the greatest geographic occupancy and were common among widespread
species ([Zobel et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.19566)). This is
consistent with Moora’s FM/inflexible distinction, but it is a record-based
proxy: unequal sampling and differences among habitats can make a species
appear flexible without a replicated within-species test of growth with and
without AMF. The study did not report a general annual/perennial relationship
that would justify assigning flexibility from lifespan.

A complementary phylogenetic experiment in *Plantago* found that arbuscule
colonisation intensity declined with phylogenetic branch length and that species
differed strongly in AM association and defence responses ([Eserman et al.
2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcad018)). This establishes phylogenetic
structure in a mycorrhizal trait and response, not a lifespan effect; it
reinforces the need to control shared ancestry before attributing status or
flexibility patterns to life history.

The annual/perennial ecotype experiment in yellow monkeyflower is a useful
within-species qualification. A coastal perennial ecotype had higher AMF
colonisation than a montane annual ecotype, but microbial effects on plant
performance were largely negative, especially in the annual, and the authors
did not demonstrate Moora-defined flexibility ([McIntosh et al.
2024](https://doi.org/10.1086/730540)). Thus higher colonisation in the
perennial ecotype did not imply greater functional dependence.

## Is colonisation rate a useful functional metric?

Colonisation rate is useful as a **state/association metric** and as a starting
point for estimating Moora’s community-level proxies, but it is a weak standalone
metric of plant benefit, dependency, or exchange. The strongest direct evidence
is mixed:

* In intact pasture soil cores, ^32P uptake through AM fungal hyphae was related
  to hyphal length in root-free soil in four of five soils, but not to either
  fractional root colonisation or colonised root length. Uptake was negligible
  in the fifth soil. Benomyl suppression reduced ^32P uptake, and inoculation
  increased clover dry mass in steamed soils, showing that fungal function can
  be detected even when root-colonisation extent is not predictive ([Jakobsen
  et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1469-8137.2001.00006.x)). This is a
  direct decoupling of root occupancy from external-hyphal P transport.
* In wheat, inoculation produced about 57% colonisation at low added P and
  35–43% with P additions, but AMF did not increase early total P uptake and
  sometimes depressed shoot growth and total P uptake. The authors explicitly
  note that percentage colonisation is not strongly correlated with growth or P
  uptake ([Li et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2006.01846.x)).
  Colonisation here tracked soil P conditions more clearly than plant benefit.
* A quantitative synthesis of experiments that manipulated colonisation found
  that biomass and host-P response ratios increased exponentially with the
  change in percent root-length colonisation, but the change explained only a
  fraction of variation and effects per unit colonisation differed among AMF
  taxa and plant functional groups ([Treseder 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11104-013-1681-5)).
  This supports colonisation as a noisy, condition-dependent correlate rather
  than a sufficient functional endpoint.

The practical hierarchy is therefore: **growth difference** for dependency;
**repeated natural occurrence across individuals/sites** for status;
**successful performance with and without AMF across relevant conditions** for
flexibility; and **colonisation plus hyphal activity, nutrient transfer, plant
nutrient content, and growth** for mechanistic validation. If only colonisation
is available, report it as a proxy and do not label it dependency.

## Evidence map

| Outcome | Best evidence located | Relation to lifespan | Directness and limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moora-defined dependency | Annual/perennial grass inoculation experiments; tallgrass prairie common-garden inoculations | Perennials often more responsive in those systems, but effects depend on P, fungus, guild, and phenology | Direct growth contrast, but few species or confounded functional groups; no measured maximum lifespan |
| Mycorrhizal status (OM/FM/NM) | Phylogenetically corrected Central European flora dataset | Association with lifespan in full dataset, null in high-confidence core dataset | Direct categorical status, but uneven observation and no growth contrast |
| Flexibility (FM/inflexible) | Global record-based status compilation; within-species/ecotype comparisons | No robust general lifespan relationship found | Proxy from occurrence records; cannot establish ability to grow without AMF |
| Root colonisation | Annual/perennial field comparisons and inoculation experiments | Direction changes among floras; annuals can be more or less colonised than perennials | Direct association measure, not functional benefit |
| P uptake/transfer | ^32P root-free compartments and AMF inoculation studies | Not tested as a lifespan relationship in the identified studies | Stronger functional endpoint; can be decoupled from colonisation percentage |
| Plant biomass/growth | Boerner, Wilson & Hartnett, Treseder synthesis | Conditional perennial advantage in some datasets | Closest to dependency, but treatment duration and partners matter |
| Phylogenetic structure | *Plantago* comparative experiment and large status datasets | Shared ancestry can explain apparent life-history patterns | Important control; does not itself establish causation |

The evidence map separates direct evidence from proxies and inference: only a
matched mycorrhizal/nonmycorrhizal growth contrast is treated as direct evidence
for Moora-defined dependency; status and flexibility require their own
observational or experimental criteria; colonisation is not upgraded to a
functional claim without a transfer or performance measurement.

## Why a lifespan effect is plausible but not sufficient

Longer-lived plants could gain an advantage from AMF when colonisation and
external mycelium take time to establish, when benefits arrive after an early
carbon cost, or when fungal infrastructure can be reused across seasons. These
are mechanistic hypotheses rather than demonstrated general lifespan effects.
Original time-course studies show that substantial AM colonisation can arise
within weeks in individual hosts ([Afek et al.
1990](https://doi.org/10.21273/JASHS.115.6.938); [Vangelisti et al.
2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-017-1211-1)), while a fungal-mycelium
experiment found viability and colonisation capacity persisting after host
shoot removal for months ([Pepe et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-28354-5)).
These findings make establishment lag and network carry-over plausible
mediators, but they do not show that a species’ evolutionary lifespan causes a
larger growth difference.

Root traits provide an important alternative explanation. The annual species
in Roumet et al. had a more acquisitive root economic strategy, whereas other
annual–perennial floras showed the reverse colonisation pattern. Across a broad
root-trait dataset, fungal collaboration and root economics were related but
distinct axes; root lifespan should not be treated as equivalent to fungal
outsourcing ([Bergmann et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aba3756)).

## Evidence limits

- **Direct lifespan evidence is sparse.** The strongest direct tests use
  annual/perennial categories, not measured maximum or median species lifespan.
- **Most comparisons are confounded.** Lifespan covaries with phylogeny,
  phenology, C3/C4 pathway, root morphology, disturbance, successional status,
  and storage or clonal organs.
- **Dependency is environment-specific.** AMF effects depend on P supply,
  inoculum, fungal isolate, soil, host genotype, harvest time, and whether
  growth, reproduction, or survival is measured.
- **Colonisation is not benefit.** Visible hyphae or vesicles may be inactive,
  and active exchange can vary without a proportional change in colonisation.
  External hyphal length/activity and isotope-based nutrient transfer are more
  informative functional additions than simply collecting more root scores.
- **No lifetime-flux result was found.** The identified primary studies do not
  measure lifespan together with integrated plant-to-fungus carbon and
  fungus-to-plant nutrient flux over a complete lifecycle.

## Implications for MycorMARL

The model should not encode a universal rule such as “perennials are more
dependent,” “longer-lived plants trade more,” or “FM species have higher
colonisation.” A defensible implementation is
to treat lifespan as a boundary condition on the time available for costs,
benefits, reproduction, and reuse of fungal infrastructure, while allowing
instantaneous dependency/benefit to depend on current P, root strategy, host,
fungus, and establishment state.

For the repo’s one-season plant–fungus setting, the useful experimental design
is to hold uptake traits and partners fixed while varying termination or
reproductive timing. Then vary AM establishment delay and whether external
mycelium persists between host generations. Record both the growth difference
with versus without AMF and cumulative C/P flows. Track colonisation as an
internal state, but validate it against external hyphal length/activity and P
transfer; do not use colonisation alone as the dependency or flexibility target.
This keeps the model aligned with Moora’s definitions
and makes any apparent lifespan effect an emergent result rather than a baked-in
coefficient. The existing related note remains at
[`plant-lifespan-and-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-investment.md`](plant-lifespan-and-arbuscular-mycorrhizal-investment.md).
