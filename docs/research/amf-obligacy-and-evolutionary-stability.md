# AMF obligacy and evolutionary stability without assumed policing

**Research date:** 17 August 2026  
**Scope:** What the host-carbon dependence of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi
(AMF) does and does not imply for evolutionary stability, particularly when
sanctions, partner choice and partner-contingent exchange are not assumed.

## Executive answer

AMF obligacy creates **dependence, not cooperation**. Under natural conditions,
fungal growth and reproduction depend on reduced carbon and lipids normally
obtained from living plants. Selection therefore favours fungal access to
hosts, but it does not necessarily favour phosphorus (P) transfer to any
particular plant. An obligately dependent fungus can remain a low-quality
mutualist or a parasite.

Without partner-specific policing, C-for-P exchange can nevertheless be stable
through **passive fitness feedback**. In a persistent one-plant--one-fungus
association, fungal P transfer may increase plant growth, which enlarges the
future carbon pool available to the same fungus. No partner assessment is
required. Stability then requires the future fungal benefit from increased
host carbon to exceed the fungal cost of P acquisition and transfer.

This feedback is strongest in a locked pair and weaker in many-to-many
associations. When several fungi share a plant, the carbon benefit created by
one contributor may also support lower-contributing fungi. When one fungus
colonises several plants, alternative hosts buffer it against decline of any
one partner. AMF may consequently be obligate on the **host community** without
being mutually dependent on each focal plant.

The defensible conclusion is therefore conditional:

> AMF obligacy can strengthen passive alignment between fungal and plant
> fitness, but evolutionary stability additionally requires the fungus that
> bears the cost of helping a plant to recapture enough of the resulting
> benefit. Obligate dependence alone does not guarantee this alignment.

## 1. What obligacy establishes

Host-derived fatty acids are causally important to normal AMF development.
Disrupting plant lipid synthesis or export impairs arbuscules and fungal growth
([Jiang et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aam9970);
[Keymer et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.29107);
[Luginbuehl et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aan0081)). Obligate
biotrophy should not, however, be interpreted as an absolute biochemical need
for physical plant contact: supplied fatty acids can support asymbiotic
branching and formation of infection-competent spores in some cultured AMF
([Kameoka et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41564-019-0485-7);
[Sugiura et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2006948117)). The relevant
ecological claim is that AMF normally depend on host-derived reduced carbon and
have little or no free-living carbon-acquisition alternative.

This dependence has three implications:

1. fungal fitness is sensitive to the abundance and persistence of suitable
   hosts;
2. severe damage to a host can reduce the fungus's future carbon source; and
3. loss of host access can produce fungal failure or coextinction.

None implies that P provision is advantageous to the fungus. A fungal variant
that retains more P while preserving access to carbon may be favoured if its
private saving exceeds the future cost of reduced host productivity. Fungal
success and plant benefit are empirically not always aligned
([Bever 2002](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2002.2162)), and AMF communities
selected under long-term fertilisation can impose greater net costs on plants
([Johnson 1993](https://doi.org/10.2307/1942106)).

## 2. What a no-policing assumption excludes

Here, policing includes any response that uses an identifiable partner's
performance to alter that partner's fitness: preferential allocation,
sanctioning, early termination of a low-performing interface, or screening
based on expected exchange quality.

The assumption does not exclude ordinary physiology. Resource fluxes may still
depend on biomass, source pools, sink strength, nutrient limitation and
transporter kinetics. A P-replete plant may grow larger and consequently
supply more total carbon without detecting or rewarding a cooperative fungus.
This is passive ecological feedback, not policing.

Correlated C and P fluxes therefore do not uniquely identify a biological
market. Increasing carbohydrate availability can stimulate fungal P uptake
and transfer through metabolic coupling
([Bücking & Shachar-Hill 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2004.01274.x)).
Likewise, experiments interpreted as reciprocal rewards demonstrate allocation
responses in simplified systems but do not by themselves establish
partner-specific policing in field networks
([Kiers et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1208473)). A broader set of
plant--AMF combinations found an association between P delivery and carbon
received, while within-plant evidence for preferential allocation was
equivocal ([Weber et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0292811)).

## 3. Stability without policing

### Passive fitness feedback

In a repeated pairwise association,

`fungal P transfer -> plant performance -> future plant C -> fungal fitness`.

Transfer can be favoured when the same fungal lineage captures enough of the
additional carbon produced by the improved host. A recent coevolutionary model
found stable one-to-one C-for-P mutualism across part of its parameter space
without partner choice or sanctions. The model also produced parasitism and
extinction, demonstrating that stability depends on resource-acquisition
efficiencies and limitation rather than following automatically from obligacy
([Grasso et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.70540)).

### Physiological or by-product coupling

Cooperation is less vulnerable to lower-contributing mutants if partner benefit
is an unavoidable by-product of a trait that directly benefits the actor. P
transfer might, for example, be coupled to fungal P homeostasis, arbuscule
function or continued acquisition of plant resources. AM symbiosis has
specialised P-transfer machinery, and loss of the plant transporter `MtPT4`
disrupts both P uptake and normal arbuscule persistence
([Harrison et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1105/tpc.004861);
[Javot et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0608136104)). This demonstrates
physiological coupling at the interface, but not that a fitness-relevant amount
of fungal P transfer is unavoidable or resistant to invasion by a
lower-transfer mutant.

### Assortment and ecological sorting

Spatial structure and partner fidelity can cause fungal lineages to experience
more of the consequences of their own effects on host performance. Mixing can
instead allow low-contributing fungi to share benefits generated by others.
Experiments show that fungal spatial arrangement affects the relative success
of beneficial and nonbeneficial fungi, although these studies do not isolate
spatial structure from host allocation responses
([Bever et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1461-0248.2008.01254.x);
[Hopkins et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1086/722532)). Networks of more-related
AMF have also transported more P and benefited plants more than networks of
less-related fungi, consistent with competition diluting cooperative returns
([van 't Padje et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.13947)).

Environmental filtering and differential growth can additionally produce
productive plant--fungus combinations without partner choice. Such ecological
sorting can maintain mutualistic associations at community level while poor
pairings decline, but it does not demonstrate stable costly exchange within
every pairing.

## 4. Why partner number changes the evolutionary problem

### One plant and one fungus

A locked pair maximises passive fitness alignment. The fungus that improves
plant performance is positioned to capture the resulting carbon, and there
are no fungal free riders or alternative hosts. It is therefore a useful but
favourable sufficiency test:

> Can resource specialisation, obligate fungal carbon dependence and repeated
> physiological feedback generate mutually beneficial C-for-P exchange without
> partner-contingent control?

A positive result would show that policing is not logically necessary under
the modelled conditions. It would not show that the exchange resists invasion
in a mixed community.

### Many-to-many associations

With multiple fungi per plant, P supplied by fungus *i* can increase a plant
carbon pool available to fungi *i*, *j* and *k*. The contributor bears the full
cost but recaptures only part of the host-level return. With multiple plants
per fungus, alternative carbon sources weaken the consequences of poor
performance toward any one host. Network-level persistence can therefore
coexist with unequal or locally parasitic exchanges.

The ancient persistence of the AM interaction class is consequently not proof
that every pairwise association or C-for-P exchange rate is evolutionarily
stable. Plant lineages have repeatedly abandoned AM, with persistent losses
associated mainly with alternative symbionts or nutrient-acquisition
strategies ([Werner et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1721629115)).
This suggests that AM often supplies a difficult-to-replace service, not that
fungal obligacy forces plants to retain the association.

## 5. Implications for modelling and inference

AMF obligacy is represented most directly by making fungal growth and
reproduction depend strongly on plant-derived carbon or lipid, with a near-zero
independent-carbon outside option. It does not justify forcing fungal P
transfer or making plant C allocation contingent on observed fungal transfer.

A no-policing model should distinguish:

- freely chosen P transfer, which permits a test of defection;
- transfer determined by fungal P state or source--sink dynamics;
- fixed stoichiometric transfer, which assumes defection is physiologically
  inaccessible; and
- policies that observe a partner's previous transfer, which implement
  contingent reciprocity even if they are not labelled sanctions.

Positive lifetime outcomes for one learned pair demonstrate ecological mutual
benefit, not evolutionary stability. The latter requires heritable transfer
variation, differential reproduction and a rare-mutant invasion or
multigenerational selection test. A useful progression is therefore to:

1. test whether pairwise exchange produces positive lifetime feedback without
   observing partner actions;
2. introduce heritable lower-transfer variants and test invasion;
3. compare freely chosen, state-dependent and physiologically coupled transfer;
   and
4. add partners to measure how shared benefits and alternative hosts alter
   pairwise alignment.

The pairwise model is thus best described as a **minimal, optimistic baseline**
for asking whether passive resource feedback can sustain C-for-P mutualism in
the absence of policing.
