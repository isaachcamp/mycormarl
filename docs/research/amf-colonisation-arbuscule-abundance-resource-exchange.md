# AM fungal colonisation, arbuscules, and resource-exchange rate

## Scope and bottom line

Question: how much can we say that the amount of root colonisation and the number/abundance of arbuscules affect the rate of reciprocal resource exchange in arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) symbioses?

**Bottom line.** Root colonisation percentage is a weak and context-dependent predictor of exchange rate. It measures how much root length contains fungal structures, not how much active exchange interface, extraradical hyphae, or fungal transport capacity is present. In one direct-tracer study, AM-acquired `33P` showed no correlation with percentage root colonisation. Conversely, there is stronger mechanistic and experimental evidence that arbuscules are the main phosphate-exchange interface: changing arbuscule density while leaving total colonisation approximately unchanged produced a large change in `33P` delivery. The most defensible statement is therefore:

> More colonised root can increase total exchange capacity when it also means more active fungal network and more functional arbuscules, but colonisation percentage alone cannot be assigned a general multiplier. Arbuscule abundance is closer to the causal interface, yet arbuscule counts remain a proxy unless paired with isotope fluxes or transporter activity.

The evidence below is classified as **field**, **lab** (including axenic/root-organ culture and controlled chamber experiments), or **glasshouse**. “Direct” means that movement of C or mineral nutrients was measured with isotope tracing or a mass-balance flux. “Proxy” means morphology, transporter expression, growth, or tissue nutrient content was used without a direct rate measurement.

## Primary studies

### Lekberg, Hammer & Olsson (2010): arbuscule abundance tracked `33P` delivery

**Setting:** lab, monoxenic root-organ/common-mycorrhizal-network system. **Exchange endpoint:** direct `13C` and `33P` tracing.

Donor roots supplied carbon to connected receiver roots with different carbon status. Overall AM colonisation did not differ between receiver treatments, but arbuscule density was approximately **two-fold higher** in carbon-supplied roots. Those roots received approximately **10-fold more `33P`**. The same experiment found four-fold more `13C` translocated toward carbon-limited receiver roots, but much of that C remained in fungal storage lipids rather than being evidence of immediate reciprocal exchange. Thus the study supports a strong association between arbuscule abundance and P delivery, while also showing that C movement and P delivery need not scale symmetrically.

This is the clearest quantitative primary result located for arbuscule abundance: approximately 2× arbuscule density accompanied approximately 10× `33P` receipt. It is not a clean per-arbuscule causal estimate because carbon status changed fungal allocation and storage, and the result comes from a simplified root-organ system. [Lekberg, Hammer & Olsson 2010, *FEMS Microbiology Ecology* 74:336–345, DOI 10.1111/j.1574-6941.2010.00956.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1574-6941.2010.00956.x)

### Bücking & Shachar-Hill (2005): exchange rate changed without a major colonisation change

**Setting:** lab, axenic transformed-carrot root culture with *Glomus intraradices*. **Exchange endpoint:** direct `14C` and `33P` tracing.

After colonisation was established, short-term sucrose supply to the root compartment increased fungal-compartment `33P` uptake from **40.9%** of supplied label in controls to **63.4–64.7%** with sucrose—an increase of roughly **55–58%**. Carbon export to the fungal compartment also rose from **0.28% to 0.36%** of supplied label under the reported sucrose treatments. The authors explicitly note that colonisation was unlikely to have changed materially over the 3–6-day treatment, so the altered P handling was attributed mainly to metabolic/transport regulation rather than more fungal biomass or more colonised root.

This is important negative evidence against treating colonisation percentage as the exchange rate: exchange can change substantially while colonisation is already established. It also shows that carbon availability can regulate P uptake and transfer at existing interfaces. [Bücking & Shachar-Hill 2005, *New Phytologist* 165:899–912, DOI 10.1111/j.1469-8137.2004.01274.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8137.2004.01274.x)

### Charters, Sait & Field (2020): no colonisation–`33P` relationship in a whole-plant experiment

**Setting:** glasshouse, wheat inoculated with *Rhizophagus irregularis*, with aphid herbivory and elevated CO₂ treatments. **Exchange endpoint:** direct `14C` allocation and AM-fungal-acquired `33P` in shoots.

Aphid herbivory greatly reduced plant C allocation to the AM fungus, while AM-mediated `33P` uptake by plants was maintained. Across plants, the authors report **no correlation between percentage root-length colonisation and AM-acquired shoot `33P`**, and no correlation between plant C outlay and shoot `33P`. This directly cautions that total colonisation is not a reliable surrogate for nutrient-exchange rate. The experiment did not manipulate arbuscule number independently, so it cannot estimate an arbuscule effect.

The result is especially relevant to models: the same apparent colonisation level can support different C-for-P exchange states, and exchange can become asymmetric. [Charters, Sait & Field 2020, *Current Biology* 30:1801–1808.e5, DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2020.02.087](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2020.02.087)

### Lekberg et al. (2024): direct exchange differed where arbuscules differed, but not a causal test

**Setting:** glasshouse incubation of intact plant communities collected from field soils. **Exchange endpoint:** direct `32P` transfer from AM fungi to shoots and `13C` transfer from shoots to AMF-specific fatty acids.

Communities from soils differing by approximately 10-fold in labile inorganic P showed different `32P`-for-`13C` exchange ratios. Within communities, grass roots had higher fungal `13C` concentration and a trend toward **more vesicles but fewer arbuscules** than blanketflower roots. The authors interpret this as consistent with different exchange costs, but the study does not provide a defensible universal slope of exchange rate against arbuscule abundance: plant identity, AMF community composition, soil history, and nutrient context all covaried.

This is direct exchange-rate evidence with arbuscule abundance measured, but the arbuscule result is **correlational**, not an independent manipulation. [Lekberg et al. 2024, *New Phytologist* 242:1576–1588, DOI 10.1111/nph.19501](https://doi.org/10.1111/nph.19501)

### Lendenmann et al. (2011): fungal identity changed fluxes more than a single colonisation metric can capture

**Setting:** glasshouse, *Medicago truncatula* with three AMF species. **Exchange endpoint:** direct `33P` and `13C` isotope tracing.

*G. intraradices* supported the greatest plant P acquisition but also imposed high C costs; *G. claroideum* delivered less P at lower C cost; *Gigaspora margarita* imposed a large C requirement with negligible P benefit. This demonstrates large functional differences among fungi under comparable host conditions. It is relevant to colonisation metrics because a percentage of colonised root does not encode fungal identity, extraradical foraging, transporter activity, or P delivery efficiency.

The paper is not a direct test of arbuscule number, so it is best used as a caveat against a one-dimensional colonisation-to-flux rule. [Lendenmann et al. 2011, *Mycorrhiza* 21:689–702, DOI 10.1007/s00572-011-0371-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00572-011-0371-5)

### Jakobsen et al. (2001): field-derived soil, but glasshouse measurement of hyphal P transport

**Setting:** glasshouse experiment using intact field soil cores from six pasture sites; additional steamed-soil inoculation experiment. **Exchange endpoint:** direct `32P` uptake from a root-free labelled compartment.

The study measured hyphal P transport, root colonisation, and shoot P in field-derived soils, with benomyl used to suppress AM fungi in intact cores. It is useful evidence that AM hyphal transport varies across real soils and that field-soil context matters. However, it does not establish a general quantitative relationship between root colonisation percentage and exchange rate; soil chemistry, indigenous AMF communities, hyphal growth, and fungicide effects are confounded with colonisation.

Classification matters: this is **not a field exchange-rate measurement**. Plants were grown and labelled in a glasshouse, although the soil cores originated from the field. [Jakobsen et al. 2001, *New Phytologist*, DOI 10.1046/j.1469-8137.2001.00006.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1469-8137.2001.00006.x)

### Field studies: colonisation and P uptake are often proxies, not exchange rates

There are genuine field studies linking AM colonisation to plant P acquisition, including a three-year field study of coexisting plant species. Such studies are valuable for ecological relevance, but plant P content or growth is not the same as instantaneous reciprocal exchange rate: it integrates soil P supply, root uptake, fungal identity, plant demand, and time. The field literature therefore supports the plausibility that greater functional AM abundance can contribute to P acquisition, but it does not justify a universal conversion such as “1% more colonisation = X% faster exchange.” [*Arbuscular mycorrhizal colonization and phosphorus acquisition of plants: effects of coexisting plant species*, 2004, DOI 10.1016/j.apsoil.2004.06.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apsoil.2004.06.002)

## Mechanistic evidence for arbuscules: strong, but usually a functional proxy

Arbuscules create the highly branched plant–fungus interface and are associated with the plant phosphate transporter MtPT4. Harrison, Dewbre & Liu showed that MtPT4 is expressed only in mycorrhizal roots, localises to arbuscule-containing cells, and has phosphate-transport properties consistent with uptake of fungal-released phosphate. Javot et al. then showed that loss of MtPT4 causes defective symbiosis: arbuscules form but collapse prematurely and overall colonisation declines. These are strong causal demonstrations that functional arbuscule interfaces are required for normal P exchange, but transporter expression, arbuscule morphology, and colonisation are still not measurements of a molar exchange rate.

[Harrison, Dewbre & Liu 2002, *The Plant Cell* 14:2413–2423, DOI 10.1105/tpc.004861](https://doi.org/10.1105/tpc.004861); [Javot et al. 2007, *PNAS* 104:1720–1725, DOI 10.1073/pnas.0610937104](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0610937104)

## What can be parameterised defensibly?

For a model, the evidence supports a hierarchy rather than one universal coefficient:

| Quantity | Evidence-based interpretation | Suggested modelling treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Total root-length colonisation | Coarse proxy for fungal presence; sometimes unrelated to measured `33P` transfer | Do not use as the sole exchange-rate multiplier; at most use as a saturating capacity term |
| Arbuscule abundance/density | Better proxy for active exchange interface; one study found ~2× arbuscules with ~10× `33P` receipt | Use as a positive capacity term, but calibrate the exponent and allow large context dependence |
| Extraradical hyphal/network surface area | Mechanistically closer to soil P acquisition capacity | Prefer when available; pair with a transfer-efficiency parameter |
| Transporter activity / arbuscule lifetime | Proxies for functional interface and regulation | Use to modulate flux, not as flux itself, unless isotope-calibrated |
| Isotope-derived C and P flux | Direct exchange-rate measurement | Preferred target for fitting and validation |

The observed ~10-fold `33P` difference for ~2-fold arbuscule density is a defensible **study-specific effect size**, not a transferable universal exponent. A simple model could test `P_exchange ∝ arbuscule_density^β` with β spanning 1–3 as a sensitivity analysis, but that range is a modelling hypothesis, not a literature-estimated consensus. A safer default is to treat arbuscule abundance as one multiplicative factor whose coefficient is fitted to isotope data and whose value can vary with host, AMF identity, P status, C supply, and soil.

## Key caveats

1. Root colonisation, arbuscule percentage, hyphal length, fungal biomass, and active exchange interface are different denominators.
2. Many “P uptake” studies measure plant tissue accumulation over days or weeks, not a rate across the interface.
3. More arbuscules may reflect greater host C supply or P demand rather than independently causing faster exchange.
4. Arbuscules are transient; a snapshot count can miss turnover and lifetime.
5. Field relevance remains the major gap: direct isotope exchange measurements with simultaneous quantitative arbuscule counts in undisturbed field conditions are scarce.
6. Most strong quantitative evidence here is from lab or glasshouse systems, often with one host and one fungal isolate; extrapolation to diverse field AMF communities is uncertain.

## Overall conclusion

We can say with moderate confidence that **functional arbuscule abundance affects potential P-for-C exchange capacity**, and with lower confidence that **total root colonisation rate alone affects exchange rate**. The best direct result located is approximately 2× arbuscule density associated with approximately 10× `33P` delivery in a lab network system. The best counter-evidence is a glasshouse isotope study finding no correlation between percentage root colonisation and AM-acquired shoot `33P`, plus a lab study in which P transfer changed by roughly 55–58% after carbon supply changed while established colonisation was unlikely to change. Therefore, colonisation should be treated as a coarse state variable, arbuscule abundance as a more informative but still imperfect proxy, and isotope-calibrated C/P flux as the exchange-rate target.
